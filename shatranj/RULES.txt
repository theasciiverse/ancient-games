What survives
-------------

Shatranj is the best documented game in this collection. Its rules were
written down by the masters who played it in Baghdad in the 9th and 10th
centuries, and their books survive in later manuscripts, with hundreds of
problems and their solutions. H. J. R. Murray gathered them in his History
of Chess (Murray 1913), and this chapter follows him throughout. What is not
known is how the game began.

India. The game’s name comes from the Sanskrit chaturanga, “of four
members”: the four arms of an Indian army, chariots, horse, elephants and
foot, a word for the army long before it was a game (Murray 1913, 42-44).
The first sure mention of the game is in Bana’s life of the king Harsha, who
ruled from 606 to 648: under him “only ashtapadas teach the positions of the
chaturanga”, the joke being that the only armies drawn up in his peaceful
realm were the ones on the board (Murray 1913, 52). Murray found no other
Indian reference before the year 1000 (Murray 1913, 56), and quotes Fiske on
all that came before the 7th century: “it is all impenetrable darkness”
(Murray 1913, 48). Dates for the invention, a Gupta origin, a fifth century,
are guesses; no source read for this book gives one.

Persia. The Persian Karnamak of Artakhshir, probably written about 600, says
its hero became “more skilled than them all in ball-play, in horsemanship,
in chess (chatrang), in hunting”, the earliest reference to chess in any
literature (Murray 1913, 149). A Persian text of the first centuries of
Islam, the Chatrang-namag, tells how a king of India sent the game to
Khusraw I (531-579) to test the wisdom of the Persians, and how the sage
Wazurgmihr worked it out and answered with a game of his own, nard (Murray
1913, 150-53); Firdausi retells the story in the Shahnama (1010), where the
board comes with men “half … of ivory, and bright, the other half of teak”
(Firdausī 1915, 7:385). Murray takes the embassies for invention, but the
tradition behind them, that chess came to Persia from India in the 6th
century, for history (Murray 1913, 153-54).

Islam. The Arabs learned the game when they conquered Persia and called it
shatranj. Al-’Adli wrote on it about 840; as-Suli, who rose at the court of
the caliph al-Muktafi (902-908) and died at Basra in 946, was its greatest
master, and the caliph ar-Radi said “As-Suli’s skill at chess charms me more
than these flowers” (Murray 1913, 199-200). Their books, and those of their
pupils, give the moves, the endings and their verdicts, the openings, and
problems (mansubat): over 1,600 in the manuscripts, about 553 of them
different (Murray 1913, 220-339, 270). Muslim chessmen were abstract shapes,
not figures (Murray 1913, 223-24).

[Chess set of glazed stonepaste, Nishapur, Iran, 12th century: the abstract
men of Islamic chess. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York,
1971.193a-ff, CC0.] From Islam the game went on to Europe, where in time the
firzan and the fil became the queen and the bishop.

Chaturanga. Of the rules of the Indian game before the year 1000, only what
al-’Adli wrote of it survives: the Persians, he says, “altered some of the
rules”, and he names the Indian ones (below) (Murray 1913, 56-57).
Al-Biruni, who was in India about 1030, describes a four-handed game with
dice and a different elephant (al-Bīrūnī 1888, 1:183-85). Bell’s chapter on
“shaturanga” (Bell 1969, 1:51-56) follows Forbes (Forbes 1860) in deriving
chess from that four-handed game and in placing its text in the Bhavishya
Purana; Murray found the passage in no manuscript of that Purana, and traces
it only to a work of about 1500 (Murray 1913, 44-49). This book follows
Murray.

The men and the board
---------------------

The board is the chessboard of eight by eight, but plain: the old boards
were not chequered (Murray 1913, 220), and the program draws them as a cloth
ruled into squares. The manuscripts colour the sides red and black,
“probably because inks of these colours were most easily procurable” (Murray
1913, 224), and so does the program. The squares are named as in chess
today, a1 at Red’s left.

    R rukh, P foot soldier (baidaq). Red in capitals.

The manuscripts draw the shahs on the d file, facing each other, the firzans
beside them (Murray 1913, 224, 247). On a plain board that is the same game
as the shahs on e1 and e8, seen in a mirror; the program sets them on the e
file, as Murray writes his moves (Murray 1913, 247).

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Man                                   Moves
  ------------------------------------- -------------------------------------
  Shah (K)                              One square any way. He may not move
                                        into check, and must be got out of it
                                        (Murray 1913, 225)

  Firzan (F)                            One square diagonally; it can reach
                                        only half the board (Murray 1913,
                                        225)

  Fil (E)                               A leap of exactly two squares
                                        diagonally, “over a diagonally
                                        adjacent square, whether occupied or
                                        empty”; each fil reaches only eight
                                        squares (Murray 1913, 225-26)

  Faras (N)                             The knight’s leap (Murray 1913, 226)

  Rukh (R)                              Any distance along a rank or file
                                        (Murray 1913, 226)

  Baidaq (P)                            One square forward, never two; takes
                                        one square diagonally forward; on the
                                        far rank it becomes a firzan, and
                                        only a firzan (Murray 1913, 226)
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The letters are the program’s: K, N and R as in chess today, F for the
firzan and E for the elephant. With so weak a firzan and fil, games are slow
to come to grips, and many are decided in the ending.

Shatranj
--------

The game of al-’Adli and as-Suli, as Murray gives it (Murray 1913, 220-339).

- Mate (shah mat) wins (Murray 1913, 225).
- Stalemate loses for the side stalemated. “The player who found himself in
  this predicament was held to have lost the game”; Murray has it from the
  solutions of the problems (Murray 1913, 229 and n. 12). Bell, after
  Forbes, gives a rule by which a stalemated shah changes places with one of
  his men and plays on (Bell 1969, 1:60); Murray shows that Forbes took it
  from Timur’s chess, a later and much changed game (Murray 1913, 229
  n. 12). The program follows Murray.
- A bare shah loses: “obtained whenever a player captured the whole of his
  opponent’s army, the King excepted, and still retained some of his own men
  upon the board … If the solitary King could take his opponent’s last
  remaining man in his move following that in which he was bared, the game
  was considered drawn” (Murray 1913, 228-29).
- Draws: by equal or too little force, by perpetual check, or by repeating
  the moves (Murray 1913, 229, 268).

Our choices. The sources say neither who moves first nor how many
repetitions make a draw, and give no count of moves for a game that goes
nowhere.

- Red moves first.
- A position reached for the third time, the same side to move, is a draw;
  this covers perpetual check.
- After 70 moves each with nothing taken and no baidaq moved, the game is a
  draw. The endings of equal force, rukh against rukh or firzans that can
  never meet, would otherwise never end.
- When a shah is bared and could bare back, the game goes on one move, and
  his side decides; the computer always takes the chance.
- A move that bares and mates at once wins as mate.

Dilaram’s problem. The most famous of the problems, as-Suli’s, copied into
Alfonso X’s Book of Games (1283) and the source of nearly 200 problems in
medieval Europe (Murray 1913, 311-12). A player, the story goes, had staked
his favourite, Dilaram, on the game, and when this position came she cried
out to him “Sacrifice your two Rooks, and not me” (Murray 1913, 311). The
program checks every move of the solution.

Shatranj, the Medinese victory
------------------------------

Al-’Adli: “Of the Indian rules of chess, one is observed by the people of
Hijaz, and is called by them the Medinese Victory. If there be with the
Kings two pieces, and the King can take a piece, then which ever first
takes, so that the other is left with nothing, wins” (Murray 1913, 57).
“There a solitary King was defeated the instant that he was bared, whether
he could bare his opponent the following move or not” (Murray 1913, 229).
Everything else is as in shatranj.

Chaturanga, the Indian rules
----------------------------

The two-handed Indian game as far as it can be had: shatranj with the rules
al-’Adli calls Indian (Murray 1913, 56-57, 60-61).

- Stalemate wins for the stalemated: “when the King cannot find a square
  into which to move, and the other King has nothing wherewith to checkmate
  him, the first has won. But this is not a Persian rule” (Murray 1913, 57).
- Baring wins for whoever bares first, as in Medina (Murray 1913, 57).
- The elephant. Murray found three moves recorded for it in India; the
  diagonal leap of two squares “became the widest spread, and it is probable
  that it is the oldest move. It is the only one which passed westwards”
  (Murray 1913, 59-60). The elephant of the ivory set once called
  Charlemagne’s, made in southern Italy in the late 11th century, is still
  an elephant (pictured). This rule set uses it; the two others are the next
  two rule sets.

Everything else (the setup, the baidaq’s promotion, who moves first, the
draws) is shatranj, our choice: no Indian text gives it.

The elephant in the corner
--------------------------

Al-’Adli: “Another Indian rule is that the Elephant is placed in the corner,
and omits one square in a straight line to jump into the second in a
straight line. And this it does in all the squares of the board. Each
Elephant has 16 squares” (Murray 1913, 57). With the elephant in the corner,
the piece with the rook’s move goes where the shatranj fil stands (Murray
1913, 60).

Al-Biruni’s elephant
--------------------

Al-Biruni, about 1030: “they move the elephant straight on, not to the other
sides, one square at a time, like the pawn, and to the four corners also one
square at a time, like the queen (firzan). They say that these five squares
… are the places occupied by the trunk and the four feet of the elephant”
(al-Bīrūnī 1888, 1:183).

Not offered: chaturaji
----------------------

Al-Biruni also describes a game for four, each with an army in a corner of
the board, the man to move chosen by the throw of dice (al-Bīrūnī 1888,
1:183-85); Raghunandana’s Tithitattva, written in Bengal about 1500, gives
more of it (Murray 1913, 68-73). So much of it is unknown (how the turn
passes, how the two dice are used, how pawns promote, how the game ends)
that it is not offered here.

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start shatranj, choose the rules, whom to play against (the computer, easy,
normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side you play.

  Key                            
  ------------------------------ -----------------------------------------------
  Arrows                         Move the cursor
  Enter or Space                 Pick up a man; put it on a lit square
  Esc                            Let go of the man
  Mouse                          Click a man, then a square
  F1 or ?                        The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc closes them)
  Enter or Space, after a game   Play again
  M, or Esc after a game         Back to the menu
  Q                              Quit

The computer searches ahead, captures to the end, and judges a position by
its men (the rukh worth most, then the faras; the firzan and the elephant
little), by the baidaqs’ advance and by where the men stand; with few men
left it hunts the other shah and his last men, since a bare shah loses. Easy
looks one move ahead and now and then plays any move, normal three, hard as
deep as it can in a second and a half.

shatranj --demo lets the computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a game
exactly.
