What survives
-------------

The game. Edward William Lane, who lived in Cairo in the 1820s and 1830s,
describes seega in An Account of the Manners and Customs of the Modern
Egyptians (first published 1836). “Many of the felláheen of Egypt also
frequently amuse themselves with a game called that of the ‘seega,’ which
may be described in a few words.” The board “consists of a number of holes,
generally made in the ground; most commonly, of five rows of five holes in
each, or seven rows of seven in each, or nine rows of nine in each: the
first kind is called the ‘khamsáwee seega;’ the second, the ‘seb’áwee;’ and
the third, the ‘tis’áwee’” (Lane 1890, 320-21).

The pieces. The holes are ’oyoon, eyes (one ’eyn), and the pieces kelbs,
dogs, “similar to those used in the game of the táb” (Lane 1890, 320-21):
there, “a little piece of stone, or dingy brick, about the size of a walnut”
for one player, and “a piece of red brick or tile” for the other (Lane 1890,
318). The program calls the two sides Stone and Brick after them.

How old. Lane says only that “several seegas have been cut upon the stones
on the summit of the great pyramid, by Arabs who have served as guides to
travellers” (Lane 1890, 320-21). Seega is often called an ancient Egyptian
game; this book has read no source that dates it before Lane.

Lane’s rules, in full (Lane 1890, 320-21):

  The players have each twelve “kelbs” […]. One of them places two of his
  kelbs in the ’eyns marked a, a: the other puts two of his in those marked
  b, b: they then alternately place two kelbs in any of the ’eyns that they
  may choose, excepting the central ’eyn of the seega. All the ’eyns but the
  central one being thus occupied (most of the kelbs placed at random), the
  game is commenced. The party who begins moves one of his kelbs from a
  contiguous ’eyn into the central. The other party, if the ’eyn now made
  vacant be not next to any one of those occupied by his kelbs, desires his
  adversary to give him, or open to him, a way; and the latter must do so,
  by removing, and thus losing, one of his own kelbs. This is also done on
  subsequent occasions, when required by similar circumstances. The aim of
  each party, after the first disposal of the kelbs, is to place any one of
  his kelbs in such a situation that there shall be, between it and another
  of his, one of his adversary’s kelbs. This, by so doing, he takes; and as
  long as he can immediately make another capture by such means, he does so,
  without allowing his adversary to move.-These are the only rules of the
  game.

For the larger boards, “in like manner”, there are kelbs “to occupy all the
’eyns excepting one” (Lane 1890, 321, note 447): twenty-four each on the
board of seven, forty on the board of nine.

The board
---------

This book names the holes by column, a letter from the left, and row, a
number from the top, as the program shows them beside the board; c3 is the
centre of the board of five. Lane’s figure of the khamsáwee seega marks a on
the middle hole of the top and bottom rows (c1 and c5) and b on the middle
hole of the outer columns (a3 and e3).

    Before the placing:
         a b c d e
       1 . . S . .
       2 . . . . .
       3 B . . . B
       4 . . . . .
       5 . . S . .
    Stone has set kelbs on c1 and c5 (Lane's a, a),
    Brick on a3 and e3 (b, b). Then each in turn sets
    down two, on any of the 20 empty holes but c3.

Seega of five
-------------

What Lane says is the game, and the program plays it as he gives it. He
leaves gaps; our choices fill them, as plainly as we could:

- Who places first, who moves first. Lane says neither. Stone places first
  (on a, a); Brick, who sets the last two kelbs down, makes the first move.
- Steps. Lane says only that the first move comes “from a contiguous ’eyn”.
  A move here is one step to a hole next to it along a row or a column,
  never diagonally, as the holes stand in rows.
- Taking. The enemy kelb must lie between the kelb moved and another of the
  mover’s in a row or a column, next to both. Only the kelb that moves
  takes, so a kelb may step in between two enemies unharmed. A step that
  traps two or three enemy kelbs at once takes them all.
- Taking again. “As long as he can immediately make another capture by such
  means, he does so”: after a capture, if any of his kelbs can take with one
  step, he moves again and must take; when he cannot, the turn passes.
- Opening a way. If the side to move has no step at all, the other must give
  up one of his kelbs next to one of the blocked side’s, which the blocked
  side can then step into. Lane gives the giver the choice.
- The end. Lane does not say how the game ends. A side whose kelbs are all
  taken has lost. Two kelbs can chase each other for ever, and a single kelb
  can never take, so a game in which nothing is taken or given for 40 moves
  ends there: the side with more kelbs wins, and equal numbers are a draw.

Example. Made by the program’s own rules engine each time the book is built.
S is a kelb of Stone, B of Brick, . an empty hole.

    The board of five, Stone to move.
    Position:
         a b c d e
       1 . S . . .
       2 . . B . .
       3 . . S B .
       4 . . . B S
       5 B . . . .
    Stone moves b1 to c1, taking c2.
    Stone takes again at once, and may only take:
    c3 to c4; e4 to e3.
    Stone moves e4 to e3, taking d3.
    Nothing more to take: Brick moves, 2 kelbs to 3.

Opening a way:

    Stone to move.
    Position:
         a b c d e
       1 B S S . .
       2 S . . . .
       3 . . . . .
       4 . . . . .
       5 . . . . S
    Stone moves e5 to e4, taking nothing.
    Brick's a1 is shut in. Stone must give up b1 or a2.
    Stone gives a2, and Brick moves a1 into it.

Seega of seven and of nine
--------------------------

Lane names the two larger boards and says only that they need kelbs for
every hole but one (Lane 1890, 321, note 447). His figure shows the board of
five; on the larger boards the program sets the first four kelbs on the same
places, the middle holes of the edges.

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start seega, choose a board, whom to play against (the computer, easy,
normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side you play.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Key                                   
  ------------------------------------- -------------------------------------
  Arrows                                Move the cursor

  Enter or Space                        Set a kelb down; give one up; pick
                                        one up, then put it on a lit hole

  Esc                                   Let go of the kelb

  Mouse                                 Click a hole, or a kelb and then a
                                        hole

  F1 or ?                               The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc
                                        closes them)

  Enter or Space, after a game          Play again

  M, or Esc after a game                Back to the menu

  Q                                     Quit
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The computer searches ahead and judges a position by the kelbs each side
has, then by the captures each could make next: easy one move ahead and now
and then any move at all, normal four, hard as deep as it can in a second
and a half (a quarter of that while placing).

seega --demo lets the computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a game
exactly.
