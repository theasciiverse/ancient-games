What survives
-------------

Alquerque. The first rules of the game are in the Book of Games that Alfonso
X of Castile had made in Seville in 1283. Its sixth part, the “Libro del
alquerque”, gives five games on three boards: Three, Nine and Twelve Men’s
Morris (the morris chapter of this book), a hunt called cercar la liebre,
“corner the hare”, and alquerque de doze, alquerque of twelve, the leaping
game of this chapter (Golladay 2007, 592). “Alquerque” there is the name of
the whole family, and comes from the Arabic al-qirq (Golladay 2007, 592 n.
990). Alfonso puts it between chess and the dice and tables games, since it
is played by skill like chess, its men stand on marked places like the men
of tables, and dice decide who begins (Golladay 2007, 624).

How old the game is, nobody can say. R. C. Bell gives an unfinished
alquerque board cut into the roof of the temple at Kurna in Egypt, “built
about 1400 B.C.” (Bell 1969, 1:47), after Parker’s Ancient Ceylon. Parker
prints the Kurna figures but never calls one of them an alquerque board, and
none matches Bell’s drawing (Parker 1909, 643-44); Friedrich Berger finds
many of the Kurna figures to be Coptic crosses, and says they “cannot be
dated” (Berger 2004, 15). Bell also finds the game in the Arabic Kitab
al-Aghani of the 10th century (Bell 1969, 1:47); the qirq there is, as far
as Berger’s sources show, the morris board (Berger 2004, 16). No dated
alquerque board older than Alfonso’s book is known to the sources of this
chapter.

Fanorona is the national game of Madagascar. W. Montgomery, a missionary,
found it in 1886 played “everywhere, in-doors and out of doors, in the town
and in the country, and by all classes”, its lines scratched on rocks, on
market sheds and on the paving outside the palace (Montgomery 1886, 148-49);
he wrote down the rules, a whole game “of average native skill” and a game
of the vela, the handicap game played after a defeat (Montgomery 1886,
152-56). The Malagasy royal histories that Father Callet gathered (second
edition 1878) tell the same: the setup, the board of nine and the board of
five, the vela (Callet 1878, 142-43). Both tell of a prince,
Andriantompokoindrindra, who would not leave a game to help his father in
battle and lost the kingdom; hence the proverb “Three against five, and toss
away the kingdom” (Montgomery 1886, 151). Montgomery dates the game’s
invention, by a family tradition, “perhaps some two hundred years ago”
(Montgomery 1886, 149); the date of “about 1680” in popular books rests on
that guess. Fanorona’s board is two alquerque boards side by side, and its
men take in a way found nowhere else.

In 2008 Maarten Schadd and four colleagues proved that fanorona, played
perfectly, is a draw (Schadd et al. 2008, 13).

Points are named by column, a letter from the left, and row, a number from
the bottom, White’s side. The diagonals run through every point whose column
and row are both odd or both even (a1, b2, c1, the corners and the centre);
Montgomery draws the fanorona board as eight squares of four “with the
diagonal lines in each of the eight” (Montgomery 1886, 151), and Alfonso’s
board is the same pattern on five by five (Golladay 2007, 597).

Alquerque de doze, after Alfonso X
----------------------------------

Alfonso: the board has “twenty-five places where the pieces may be placed”,
twelve men a side “in a troop formation”, and “there remains one empty place
in the center”; “the one that plays first has a disadvantage because he is
to play perforce in that empty space”. A man takes the man that moved next
to it “by jumping over it from one space to another as the straight lines on
the board go”, “and over as many pieces as he should jump in this manner he
will remove them all”. “The one who guards his pieces worse and loses them
more quickly, is defeated. And if both players know how to play it,
frequently there can be a tie” (Golladay 2007, 624).

That is all. Our choices, where Alfonso is silent:

- The middle row: each side’s two men on its own right, as Bell draws it
  (Bell 1969, 1:47); Alfonso’s own diagram could not be read.
- Moves go any way along a line; nothing in the text limits them.
- Taking is not compulsory, and a man that has taken may stop: Alfonso says
  neither. The huff, losing a man that could have taken and did not, is
  first recorded in Covarrubias’ dictionary of 1611: “in certain games of
  alquerque they call soplar taking the opponent’s piece that could have
  taken and did not” [Covarrubias Orozco (1611), s.v. soplar; our
  translation]. Sonja Musser Golladay, who translated the book, notes that
  the huff “is not explicit” in it (Golladay 2007, 593).
- No move loses. Ties: a third repetition, or 50 turns with nothing taken.

Alquerque, after Bell
---------------------

Bell gives Alfonso’s rules with “Suggested Additional Rules”, since “These
rules from the Alfonso manuscript are not sufficient to play a game” (Bell
1969, 1:48): compulsion with the huff (“If a piece can make a capture it is
forced to do so, otherwise it is huffed and is removed from the board”),
which he takes to be Alfonso’s, and three rules of his own: “No piece can
move backwards; only forwards, diagonally forwards, or sideways”; “No piece
may return to a point that it has been on before”; and “A piece reaching the
opponent’s back line is unable to move except by making a capture by a short
leap over an enemy piece”. He scores the game in points as well (Bell 1969,
1:48). Golladay calls his view that Alfonso’s rules are not enough
“incorrect” (Golladay 2007, 593).

Our choices. The program makes taking compulsory rather than huffing: Bell
does not say who takes the huffed man, or when. A man must leap on while it
can, as Bell’s “taken in the same move” has it. Bell’s rule against coming
back covers leaps too. Bell’s points are not kept. With these rules every
game ends, since no man can go back.

Fanorona, today’s rules
-----------------------

The game as Schadd and his colleagues give it, after Bell and the
Chauvicourts’ rules of 1980 Schadd et al. (2008): “Capturing moves are
obliged and have to be played, if possible, above non-capturing (paika)
moves”; a man may go on “as long as possible”, and “the player may stop
capturing after any number of opponent pieces are captured”; it may not take
“in the same direction as the capturing move directly before”, nor “arrive
on the same intersection twice during a capturing sequence” (Schadd et al.
2008, 4-6). The game is a draw “if no player is able to win”, in practice by
repetition; a player who cannot move loses, which they say no rules they
knew had settled (Schadd et al. 2008, 6). Montgomery’s account fits: every
capture in his sample game is the one these rules give (Montgomery 1886,
154).

Fanorona, after Montgomery
--------------------------

Montgomery, 1886: “At the beginning of a game one move only is permitted to
the first side. After that side has moved once, any piece that is moved is
permitted to run amuck in the enemy’s lines, and to go on as long as he
finds foes to capture, provided (a) that he does not return immediately to
any point he has just left, and (b) that he does not take a foe behind him
immediately after taking one in front of him, nor one on his right hand
immediately after taking on his left hand, and vice versa. ‘Dont eat at both
ends, like a leech,’ says the Malagasy proverb” (Montgomery 1886, 153).

Montgomery does not say that taking is compulsory. In his sample game no
player ever steps without taking when he could have taken (Montgomery 1886,
154), and Callet’s words can be read the same way (Callet 1878, 142-43).
Ayres, who sold the game in London in 1889, says it is not (F. H. Ayres
1889, 44). Our choice: compulsory, as the sample game plays it. Our reading
of (a) and (b): both forbid a step straight back along the line of the last
step; nothing else.

Vela
----

“Should one of the players have been defeated, however, he is not allowed to
play on the same footing as before” (Montgomery 1886, 154-55). The game “is
opened by the victor, who puts forward such of his pieces as he chooses to
surrender to his antagonist. These pieces may only be taken singly, and the
generous conqueror refrains from taking any of his enemy’s pieces, until he
has parted with, one by one, 17 of his own pieces; then, with the remaining
five, he begins his campaign against the undiminished forces of his
antagonist” (Montgomery 1886, 155). The loser plays the vela until he wins
one (Montgomery 1886, 155; Callet 1878, 142-43); in olden times, Montgomery
heard, the beaten player was forgiven “on condition of his kneeling down
before his conqueror and bleating like a sheep” (Montgomery 1886, 155).

Our reading. In Montgomery’s sample vela the loser moves first, and each of
his first seventeen captures takes one man only, the nearest, with no
capture after it (Montgomery 1886, 155-56); the program plays it so. The
computer, as the winner, offers its men; giving them is strategy, not a
rule.

    The vela opens: White, who lost, moves first.
    White's 5 turns all take, one man each: d2 to e3, approaching
    f4 and g5, takes only f4.
    Black now has 7 moves and may take with none of them.

Fanoron-dimy
------------

Callet names two fanoronas, “one of nine and one of five; the one of five is
a small, quick fanorona” [Callet (1878), 142-143; our tentative
translation], and the dictionary of Abinal and Malzac has andimy, the
fanorona of five lines (Abinal and Malzac 1888, s.v. andimy). No source read
for this chapter gives its setup or its rules: the program’s are by analogy,
the middle row alternating as on the board of nine, and today’s rules.
Schadd’s team solved it too: a draw (Schadd et al. 2008, 13).

Not offered
-----------

Cercar la liebre, Alfonso’s hunt of one hare against ten to twelve hunters
on the alquerque board (Golladay 2007, 625), and fanoron-telo on three by
three, which some books make a capture game and others a game of three in a
row.

The ornament
------------

The alquerque rule sets are decorated after the pictures of the game in
Alfonso X’s book of 1283 (fols. 91v to 93v). In the first, the board itself
is “edged in alternating dark and white woods” (Golladay 2007, 1024); in the
following pictures, quatrefoils of gold stand at the corners of the frame,
holding the castle of Castile and the lion of León (Golladay 2007, 760;
Historic Games 2003). The program’s stone carries the inlaid edge, and on a
terminal larger than 100 by 30 the margins carry the castle in its
quatrefoil (the lion is too small to draw in dots).

Fanorona is a living game of Madagascar; its boards are lines on a chopping
block or a winnowing platter, cut in rock or in paving (Montgomery 1886).
The Fanorona rule sets are left plain. The ornament is drawn in braille
dots, in dim tones, never over a man, a highlight or a line. O turns it off
and on, and alquerque --plain starts without it.

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start alquerque, choose the rules, whom to play against (the computer, easy,
normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side you play.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Key                                   
  ------------------------------------- -------------------------------------
  Arrows                                Move the cursor

  Enter or Space                        Pick up a man; put it on a lit point;
                                        choose the line to take

  Esc                                   Let go of the man, or end a turn of
                                        captures where it may end

  Mouse                                 Click a man, then a point

  F1 or ?                               The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc
                                        closes them)

  Enter or Space, after a game          Play again

  M, or Esc after a game                Back to the menu

  O                                     The ornament off and on

  Q                                     Quit
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The computer searches ahead and judges a position by the men each side has,
the most it could take at once, and how freely each side can move: easy one
turn ahead and now and then any move at all, normal three, hard as deep as
it can in a second and a half.

alquerque --demo lets the computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a game
exactly; --plain leaves out the ornament.
