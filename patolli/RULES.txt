What survives
-------------

No writer of the 16th century gives the rules of patolli. What the
chroniclers give is the equipment, the count of the throw, and what the game
meant to those who played it. R. C. Bell put it plainly: “Little more is
known about patolli and this is not enough to play the game” (Bell 1969,
1:7).

Diego Durán, a Dominican friar in Mexico, wrote the fullest account, about
1579, in his book of the old rites [Durán (1880), 234-238; our
translations]. On a mat “they had painted a great saltire that took the mat
from corner to corner; across the inside of the saltire were drawn lines
that served as houses”, ruled in melted rubber. “For these houses there were
twelve small stones, six red and six blue … if two played, which was the
usual way, one took six and the other the other six; and though many played,
one always played for all.” The lots were “some black beans, five or ten as
they wished to lose or win, which had little white holes, one in each bean …
if one, one; if two, two; if three, three; if four, four; but five showing
were ten, and ten, twenty”; “and so those little white spots were the lots,
and the count of the lines won, and for moving the stones from one house to
another” (Durán 1880, 2:235).

The god of the beans. “The name of the god of the dice was Macuilxochitl,
which means five roses”; there were five beans in his honour, and as they
cast them on the mat the players “called aloud ‘Macuilxochitl!’ and gave a
great clap of the hands, and then looked to see what points had come” (Durán
1880, 2:237). Gamesters carried the mat rolled under the arm and the beans
tied in a cloth, spoke to them “a thousand words of love”, and offered
incense and food before they played (Durán 1880, 2:235-36). “So many
onlookers and gamesters came that they were on top of one another over the
mat, some to play, others to bet” (Durán 1880, 2:235).

Playing oneself into slavery. Durán’s chapter is “of the games these Indians
had to amuse and divert themselves on feast days, but also to stake
themselves and become slaves for ever” (Durán 1880, 2:234): when nothing
else was left, “they staked themselves at a price, on condition that if
within a certain time they could not redeem themselves, they would remain
the slave for ever of the one who won them” (Durán 1880, 2:237). Gómara, the
first to describe the game in print (1552), and Torquemada say the same
(López de Gómara 1870, 224; Torquemada 1723, 2:554).

Bernardino de Sahagún counts patolli among the lords’ pastimes: “a game
called patolli, which is like the game of castro or alquerque, or almost
like dice; there are four large beans, each with a hole, and they throw them
by hand on a mat … where a figure is made”; elsewhere he has three beans
[Sahagún (1829), 292, 317; our translation]. The game and the ball game
“they have given up, as suspect of some idolatrous superstitions” (Sahagún
1829, 2:292); the magistrates tore the mats and “burned on their hands the
little beans that served as dice”, so that, Durán writes, no memory of the
game was left (Durán 1880, 2:238).

Moctezuma is often said to have played patolli. Gómara says only that
“sometimes Moctezuma watched them play” (López de Gómara 1870, 223); the
game Bernal Díaz saw him play with Cortés was totoloque, pellets of gold
thrown at a mark (Díaz del Castillo 1632, cap. XCVII).

What is not known. How the stones went round, what the marked compartments
did, how a game was won: nothing in the chronicles says. The pictures show
an X with arms two compartments wide, hourglass marks near the ends and
rounded ends, but they differ in the number of compartments. The 52
compartments of many modern accounts, said to match the 52 years of the
Aztec calendar round, come from 20th-century reconstructions, Mateos
Higuera’s of 1930 and Swezey’s of 1970, not from any chronicle Swezey and
Bittman (1983); so does the rule that a bean standing on its edge wins all
(Kendall 1980). The many square boards scratched in the floors of
Teotihuacan and the Maya cities, often called patolli boards, are of another
game: “none offers the cross-shaped board typical of patolli” (Depaulis
2018, 42). Edward Tylor argued in 1879 that patolli came from the Indian
pachisi (Tylor 1879, 124-25); the question has stayed open.

Bell’s figure, “after Duran” (Bell 1969, 1:6), is the board here: four arms,
each two rows of seven compartments, the last of each row half of the arm’s
rounded end, round four squares in the middle; in every row the fifth and
sixth compartments from the middle are pinched by black triangles, an
hourglass across the arm. Bell’s text gives “sixteen compartments” a limb
(Bell 1969, 1:7); his drawing agrees only if the two squares of the middle
beside each limb are counted with it. Round the outline of the cross the
compartments make a ring of 60. The program draws the cross upright; on the
mat it lay corner to corner.

Bell’s suggested rules
----------------------

Bell’s “Suggested Additional Rules (for two players)” (Bell 1969, 1:7-8), in
his words:

1.  “The players put an agreed sum into a pool and decide on the size of the
    forfeits.”
2.  “One player takes six red pieces, the other six blue.”
3.  “Each player casts the patolli in turn and the higher scorer starts the
    game by casting again.”
4.  “The opening player introduces a piece on to his nearest central square
    and then moves it the indicated number of compartments in either
    direction round the board, but whichever way his first piece moves the
    others must do likewise. His opponent at his opening throw also has a
    free choice of direction but having once chosen, the remaining pieces
    must travel the same way.”
5.  “After the entry of the first piece the others must enter the board on
    the player’s nearest central square with a throw of 1.”
6.  “No piece may move on to a compartment occupied by any other piece.”
7.  “If two or more of a player’s pieces can be moved to satisfy a
    particular throw, the player has free choice which it shall be, but if
    only one piece can move, the move must be made even if it is to the
    player’s disadvantage.”
8.  “If a player cannot move any piece he pays a forfeit into the pool.”
9.  “A player landing on a compartment which has been reduced in size by the
    markings pays two forfeits to his opponent.”
10. “A player moving a piece on to one of the rounded compartments at the
    end of the limbs of the cross is awarded another turn.”
11. “Pieces travel round the cross and are borne off on the side of the
    fourth limb nearest the player with exact throws, as in backgammon. The
    four central squares, which have been traversed in the circuit of the
    piece, are excluded in the bearing off.”
12. “As each of the player’s pieces leaves the cross his opponent pays him
    one forfeit.”
13. “The player removing all his pieces from the cross first wins the stake
    in the pool.”

Rules 8 to 10 rest on a guess, which Bell gives: “By comparison with other
North American games it is probable that the marked squares were penalty
areas and the player was penalized for trespassing on them” (Bell 1969,
1:7). His rules 14 and 15, for three and four players, are not offered.

The count of the beans is Durán’s; with five beans, each as likely to fall
hole up as not:

    Red begins, and casts 3.
    2 moves: a stone in on Red's square of the middle and 3 on, either way.
    Red takes it down its own arm first (against the sun, as the program
    draws it): every Red stone will go that way round.
    Next, a 2 can only move that stone (1 move).
    Only a 1 brings in another stone: yes.

    Red has stones 3 and 56 along its way; purses 20 and 20.
    A 2: 3 to 5, a pinched compartment. Red pays Blue 2: purses 18 and 22.
    A 2 again: 5 to 7, a rounded end: Red throws again.
    A 4 bears 56 off exactly; a 10 could not.
    Blue pays one for it: purses 19 and 21.

Our choices, where Bell is silent:

- The stakes: each puts 10 into the pool and has 20 in his purse; a forfeit
  is 1. A player who cannot pay in full pays what he has, and the game goes
  on: only bearing off ends it.
- No holes up moves nothing and costs nothing; it is not a throw that
  “cannot move” (Durán gives no value for it).
- Passing: a stone may pass over others; Bell forbids only landing.
- Another turn on a rounded end is one more cast.
- The way round: Red enters on the lower left square of the middle and Blue
  on the upper right, opposite; each chooses his way with his first stone. A
  stone goes off from the last compartment before its own square of the
  middle, which it does not enter again.

Not offered. A game on the 52-compartment route of Swezey’s reconstruction,
three arms of the cross for each player (Swezey and Bittman 1983, 375,
fig. 17b): almost everything in it but the route would be our own invention.
Kendall’s and Mateos Higuera’s rules, the likely source of today’s popular
ones, could not be read (Kendall 1980; Mateos Higuera 1930).

The ornament
------------

Durán and Sahagún both say the board was painted on a mat. In the patolli
scene of the Codex Magliabechiano (fol. 60r), the players sit on mats woven
in a herringbone of short strokes, and five gold discs are drawn above the
board (Nuttall 1903). The codex does not say what the discs mean, and the
program does not guess. Its mat carries that weave in the four quarters
beside the cross, and on a terminal larger than 100 by 30 the five discs
stand in a row above it, as in the codex. Both are drawn in braille dots, in
dim tones, never over a stone, a highlight or a number. They come from the
sixteenth-century pictures, not from the living Nahua tradition; the
step-fret often put on patolli boards today is in none of the patolli
pictures and is not used. O turns the ornament off and on, and
patolli --plain starts without it.

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start patolli, choose whom to play against (the computer, easy, normal or
hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side you play. Who begins
is cast for.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Key                                   
  ------------------------------------- -------------------------------------
  Enter or Space                        Cast the beans; send a stone to the
                                        lit compartment under the cursor

  Arrows                                Move the cursor round the cross

  B                                     Bear a stone off

  Mouse                                 Click to cast; click a lit
                                        compartment, or a stone

  F1 or ?                               The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc
                                        closes them)

  Enter or Space, after a game          Play again

  M, or Esc after a game                Back to the menu

  O                                     The ornament off and on

  Q                                     Quit
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The computer: easy moves its furthest stone, and now and then any; normal
judges the race and the purses, and likes the rounded ends; hard also weighs
every cast the other side could make next, and its best answer.

patolli --demo lets the computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a game
exactly; --plain leaves out the ornament.
