What survives
-------------

How old. Pachisi is often called the national game of India and said to be
thousands of years old: the dice game in which the Pandavas lost their
kingdom in the Mahabharata, or the game Shiva and Parvati play in the cave
temples of Ellora. Neither holds. The epic’s game is played with dice nuts
and no board; “there is not the slightest reason to ascribe the use of such
a cloth to the epic period”, as Heinrich Lüders put it [Lüders (1907), 14
n. 4; our translation]. The picture of the gods at Ellora was retouched to
show a cross-shaped board (Schmidt-Madsen 2021, 39-40). Jacob
Schmidt-Madsen, who has gone through the evidence, finds the first sure
signs of the cross-shaped game in Indian poems and paintings of the 15th and
16th centuries: “The national game of India cannot be traced beyond the 15th
century at the earliest” (Schmidt-Madsen 2021, 35-37).

Akbar. The first account of the rules is in the Ain-i Akbari, the record of
the emperor Akbar’s court that Abu’l-Fazl wrote about 1590: “From times of
old, the people of Hindústán have been fond of this game”; “Formerly many
grandees took part in this game; there were often as many as two hundred
players, and no one was allowed to go home before he had finished sixteen
games, which in some cases lasted three months. If any of them lost his
patience and got restless, he had to drink a cup of wine” (Abu’l-Fazl 1873,
1:303-4). The story that Akbar played on a board paved in a courtyard at
Fatehpur Sikri with slave girls for men is not in Abu’l-Fazl; the earliest
form of it found for this book is the translator Blochmann’s own note to his
plate, and Rousselet tells it as “It is said …” (Abu’l-Fazl 1873; Rousselet
1882, 302).

The names. In the Panjab “the game of chaupur occurs so frequently in these
legends, and its technicalities play so important a part in them” that
Richard Temple described it: with dice it is phansa, with cowries pachisi
(Temple 1884, 1:243-45). The name comes from the highest throw: “the game is
generally played with six cowries, making the highest throw 25 … hence it is
termed Pucheesee (from puchees, 25)” (Herklots 1832, liii). The book follows
Temple and Schmidt-Madsen in calling the dice game chaupar and the cowrie
game pachisi (Schmidt-Madsen 2021, 33). The first European picture of the
board is Thomas Hyde’s in 1694; the man he asked “could not teach me the way
of playing” (Hyde 1694, 69-70).

The rules, like the game, vary: “one native gives one description of the
game, and another another” (Falkener 1892, 264). The fullest early rules are
those Rajendralala Mitra of Calcutta gave Edward Tylor, printed in 1879
(Tylor 1879, 118-20); Edward Falkener’s of 1892 are the ones R. C. Bell
reprints, almost word for word (Falkener 1892, 257-62; Bell 1969, 1:9-12).
The English game of Ludo was taken from pachisi in the 1880s (Schmidt-Madsen
2021, 34).

The cross with Red’s way numbered: out down the middle row (1 to 8), round
against the sun (9 to 76) and back up the middle row (77 to 83) into the
middle. Crosses: Tylor’s castles; circles: Falkener’s; both at the end of
each middle row.

The board is a cloth, often embroidered; “zealous players often carrying one
rolled round in their turbans” (Tylor 1879, 118). All the sources agree on
the cross of four arms of three columns by eight rows, and on the way round:
out down one’s own middle row, round the outside rows “from right to left
(i.e. contrary to the sun)”, and back up the middle row into the middle
(Herklots 1832, lii; Tylor 1879, 119; Falkener 1892, 261). The program
numbers a man’s way 1 to 84 as above, from Falkener’s numbered drawing
(Falkener 1892, 263).

There are twelve castles, “the twelve privileged squares, which are marked
with a cross” (Herklots 1832, lii): the outer end of each middle row, and
two on each arm’s outer rows. Where those two stand, the sources differ:
Tylor’s drawing puts them on the fourth square from the middle (Tylor 1879,
119), Falkener’s and Bell’s on the fourth from the outer end (Falkener 1892,
263; Bell 1969, 1:10). Culin found both on real boards (Culin 1898, 854
n. 1). Each rule set keeps its own source’s.

Rules every set shares
----------------------

Our choices, where the sources are silent: the order of play, round against
the sun like the men; men passing freely over others (no source mentions
blocking a way); and two players, each with a partnership, as Falkener and
Temple describe for two players (Falkener 1892, 264; Temple 1884, 1:244),
since the program’s menu has two sides.

Pachisi, after Tylor
--------------------

The rules Rajendralala Mitra gave Tylor, “a more complete and consistent set
of rules” than earlier ones (Tylor 1879, 118). The cowries count as Herklots
and Shway Yoe also give them (Tylor 1879, 119; Herklots 1832, lii-liii;
Shway Yoe 1896, 375):

    Mouths up  Counts   Chance     Then
            0       6    1 in 64   throw again
            1      10    6 in 64   throw again; a starting 1
            2       2   15 in 64
            3       3   20 in 64
            4       4   15 in 64
            5      25    6 in 64   throw again; a starting 1
            6      12    1 in 64   throw again

- “The high throws 6, 10, 12, 25, entitle the player to a new throw … but at
  the lower throws the lead passes to the other player.”
- A 10 or a 25 brings “a starting 1 (puá) in addition to the ordinary score,
  by which 1 a-piece is put on the first square”; “the throws are useless
  till one player throws 10 or 25” (Tylor 1879, 119-20).
- “A single man on a square is taken by an enemy’s man moving on to that
  square, and the taken man being dead (mard) is put back in the home to
  start afresh”; “taking or cutting (kátá) a man gives the player a new
  throw”; “two or more men of one set on a square hold it safely, all which
  is as in our backgammon”; “the crossed squares or forts are places where a
  single man is in safety, and even blocks an enemy’s man from moving there”
  (Tylor 1879, 120).
- A man coming home “is called ripe (pakká)”; going home “a player cannot
  use a throw for which there are not sufficient squares left”, and a man on
  the last square “can only be got off the board as it got on, by a throw of
  das or pachisi” (Tylor 1879, 120 and n.).
- “If 25, 10, or 12 are thrown thrice running, they are called rotten
  (pachá) and destroy one another, but a new throw of 10 or 25 may restore
  them, and so with six consecutive throws, restored by a seventh” (Tylor
  1879, 120 n.).

    Red throws 10 (one mouth up): throw again; then 2.
    To use: 10, 1 (starting), 2.
      1: a man from the middle to square 1.
      10: a man from square 1 to square 11.
      2: a man from square 11 to square 13.
    Square 13 is a fort: the man is safe there.

Our readings and choices. Throws may be left unused (Tylor is silent;
Falkener allows it). The rotten throws: within a turn, the third (or sixth)
high throw running puts the last three in doubt; the next throw, if a 10 or
a 25, restores them, and otherwise they are struck out, and if the turn ends
on them they are lost:

    Thrown 25, 12, 10, 3: kept 3
    Thrown 25, 12, 10, 25: kept 25, 12, 10, 25, and throws on

A partner whose men are all home “can throw on his partner’s behalf, after
getting a new starting 1” (Tylor 1879, 120 n.); the program lets him throw
for his partner at once. Left out: moving two men as a couple (jora), which
Tylor says good players do not recognise, and a ripe man going round again
(Tylor 1879, 120 n.).

Pachisi, after Falkener
-----------------------

Falkener’s rules, which Bell follows (Falkener 1892, 259-62; Bell 1969,
1:9-11). His cowries count differently from Tylor’s, and a 25 is rare:

    Mouths up  Counts   Chance     Then
            0      25    1 in 64   throw again; a grace of 1
            1      10    6 in 64   throw again; a grace of 1
            2       2   15 in 64
            3       3   20 in 64
            4       4   15 in 64
            5       5    6 in 64
            6       6    1 in 64   throw again

- “In commencing the game the first piece may be entered whatever throw is
  made, but the other pieces can enter only with a grace. So, likewise, a
  piece taken up can enter only with a grace” (Falkener 1892, 261).
- The castles: “Any number of pieces of a player or of his partner are safe
  in these castles, and an enemy cannot enter”; “Pieces may double on other
  squares, but it is at their own peril … they can be taken off by a single
  piece at one stroke” (Falkener 1892, 260).
- “On taking a piece the player may throw again”; “A player may refuse to
  play when it comes to his turn, or he may throw and then refuse to take
  it” (Falkener 1892, 261).
- Falkener also notes that the castles stand 25 apart, from the middle: 0 to
  25, 21 to 46, 59 to home (Falkener 1892, 260). The program’s numbering
  bears him out.
- “Sometimes the player who is out first is permitted to give his throws to
  his partner, but this is not the game” (Falkener 1892, 262); the program
  follows “the game”.

Bell’s differences. Bell drops the grace and lets men enter on a 6, 10 or 25
(Bell 1969, 1:11); but he keeps the exact finish, so a man on the last
square, needing a 1, could never get home. The program plays Falkener’s
grace. Our reading of the grace: it lets a man out into the middle, from
where any throw moves it, as Falkener’s counting of the castles from the
middle requires.

Chaupar, after Abu’l-Fazl
-------------------------

Abu’l-Fazl describes the dice: “Four of the six sides of each dice are
greater than the remaining two, the four long sides being marked with one,
two, five, and six dots respectively”. “Generally played by four players, of
whom two play against the other two. Each player has four pieces”; he “puts
two in the sixth and seventh spaces of the middle row of the parallelogram
before him, and the other two in the seventh and eighth spaces of the right
row. The left row remains empty”. “As long as a player keeps two of his
pieces together, the adversary cannot throw them out.” “If a player throws a
double six, he can move two pieces over twelve spaces, provided the two
pieces stand together on one field … A similar rule holds for double fives,
&c.” “If a player has brought his four pieces into the central square, he
throws, when his turn comes, for his companion, to get him out too”
(Abu’l-Fazl 1873, 1:303-4).

    Red's men start on squares 6, 7, 15, 16 (Abu'l-Fazl).
    Red has men on 6, 7 and two on 20, and throws 5, 5, 2.
    The two fives may move the pair on 20 ten squares each, to 30.
    Or each die moves a man by itself: 7 plays in all.

Our readings and choices. Where the men start: Abu’l-Fazl does not say from
which end he counts; counting along the way the men go puts them on 6, 7, 15
and 16, and Falkener’s “6 and 7” agrees for the first two (Falkener 1892,
264). Each die moves a man by itself, as Falkener’s doublets “given to a
single piece” or “to two pieces” imply. A pair moved together by a doublet
can take a pair, as Falkener’s Chausar allows: “they cannot be taken except
by doubles” (Falkener 1892, 264). The throw of six, five and one Abu’l-Fazl
calls khám, with its own rule, is left out, being obscure; so are the
betting and the fines he describes (Abu’l-Fazl 1873, 1:303-4). A man taken
comes back with a die of 1, the paun, the entering throw of the cowrie game
(Temple 1884, 1:245); no source says how.

The ornament
------------

On a terminal larger than 100 by 30, the four corners beside the program’s
cross carry a flower from a painting in the Metropolitan Museum: Ambav’s
“Maharaja Sovan Singh playing pachisi”, Udaipur, about 1868 (The
Metropolitan Museum of Art 2026). The square at the centre of the painted
board holds a white flower on red: eight long pointed petals, eight short
ones between them. It is drawn in braille dots in a dim tone, never over a
man, a highlight or a number. No board earlier than the nineteenth century
was found, so this is not offered as the ornament of Akbar’s time. O turns
it off and on, and pachisi --plain starts without it.

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start pachisi, choose the rules, whom to play against (the computer, easy,
normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which partnership you
play.

  Key                            
  ------------------------------ -----------------------------------------------
  Enter or Space                 Throw; pick up a man; put it on a lit square
  Arrows                         Move the cursor round the cross
  Esc                            Let go of the man
  P                              Leave the rest of the throws unused
  Mouse                          Click to throw, to pick up and to put down
  F1 or ?                        The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc closes them)
  Enter or Space, after a game   Play again
  M, or Esc after a game         Back to the menu
  O                              The ornament off and on
  Q                              Quit

The computer plans the use of all its throws at once, trying every order and
every man: easy moves forward and takes when it can, and now and then plays
any move; normal judges where the men end by the race, the men in safety and
the enemies behind them; hard reckons how likely each exposed man is to be
hit, and plays out the next enemy turn from its best few plans. The throws
decide much.

pachisi --demo lets the computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a game
exactly; --plain leaves out the ornament.
