What survives
-------------

The name. Tafl, Old Norse for “table”, was a general word for board games.
By the end of the Viking Age the game of the king was called hnefatafl in
Scandinavia, to tell it from skáktafl (chess), kvatrutafl (tables) and
halatafl (the fox game). Hnefi, “fist”, named the king piece (Wikipedia
2026). The tafl games were played in Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Iceland,
Britain, Ireland and Sápmi, the land of the Sámi, and chess pushed them
aside from the twelfth century (Wikipedia 2026).

The boards and pieces. Boards and pieces turn up across that world. Bell
describes a board fragment from a Roman Iron Age grave at Wimose on Fyn in
Denmark, before about AD 400, with at least eighteen squares along one side,
and a board from the Gokstad ship burial in Norway, cut for Nine Men’s
Morris on one side and on the other for a game that may have been hnefatafl
(Bell 1969, 1:79, 93-94). A wooden board of seven by seven found at
Ballinderry in Ireland in 1932 has holes for pegged pieces (Wikipedia 2026);
it marks the centre and corner squares with circles (Ashton 2007, 15). A
glass piece found on Lindisfarne in 2019, topped with white droplets thought
to stand for a crown, is thought to be a piece from hnefatafl or a game like
it (Wikipedia 2026).

The sagas. The Icelandic sagas mention the game often but never explain it.
In the Orkneyinga saga Earl Rögnvald Kali Kolsson puts skill at tafl first
among his nine boasts. In Friðþjófs saga the king’s men are red and the
attackers white (Wikipedia 2026). One of the riddles in the Saga of Hervör
and Heidrek is answered with hnefatafl: the darker pieces defend the hnefi,
the white ones attack (Cartier 2011, 7; Wikipedia 2026). Whether a line of
the same riddle calls the king or his maids “weaponless” is disputed, and
with it whether the king took part in captures (Wikipedia 2026).

Other games of the family. Brandubh, “black raven”, was the Irish form: two
poems say it was played with five men against eight, one of the five a
chief, the branán. Tawlbwrdd was the Welsh form; Robert ap Ifan wrote down
an incomplete account of it with a drawing in a manuscript of 1587, on a
board of eleven by eleven with a king and twelve men against twenty-four
(Wikipedia 2026). No full rules of any of these survive.

Tablut. The one eyewitness account is the botanist Carl Linnaeus’. On his
journey through Lapland in 1732 he saw the Sámi play a game he called
Tablut, and wrote its board, its pieces and fourteen laws into his diary, in
a mix of Swedish and Latin (Bell 1969, 1:77-79; Ashton 2007, 5-6). The
defenders are eight Swedes and their king, the attackers sixteen Muscovites.
The board was a reindeer skin with embroidered squares (Bell 1969, 1:78;
Wikipedia 2026).

The translation that went wrong. The diary was first published in English in
1811, edited by James Edward Smith, with the Tablut rules translated by
Charles Troilius (Linnaeus 1811; Wikipedia 2026). Linnaeus’ ninth law says
that a piece standing between two enemies is killed, and ends item Rex,
“likewise the king”. The translation gives it as “except the king, who is
not liable to this misfortune” (Linnaeus 1811, 2:57; Ashton 2007, 6, 12). It
also leaves out the last two of the fourteen laws (Ashton 2007, 1, 14). H.
J. R. Murray built his reconstruction on that translation, and later game
historians built on Murray; a king who can only be taken by four is so
strong that his side nearly always wins (Ashton 2007, 1).

What is reconstructed. Everything played today. John C. Ashton retranslated
Linnaeus’ Latin in 2007, Nicolas Cartier in 2011 and Olli Salmi in 2013
(Ashton 2007; Cartier 2011; Wikipedia 2026); read so, the king is taken by
two like any other piece, except on and beside his castle. The modern games
grew out of the older reading: to make up for its strong king they changed
other rules, above all sending him to the corners instead of the edge;
Copenhagen builds on Fetlar (Wikipedia 2026). The program offers both lines:
the three modern rule sets, Linnaeus’ Tablut as it is now read, and R. C.
Bell’s Tablut of 1960, which follows the 1811 translation.

Copenhagen 11x11
----------------

The rule set most played in tournaments today (Wikipedia 2026). Aage
Nielsen, Adam Bartley and Tim Millar drew up the Copenhagen rules in 2012;
the program follows them as set out on Nielsen’s site (Nielsen and Bartley
2026b). These are given in full; the other rule sets are told by how they
differ.

1.  The attackers move first; then the players take turns.
2.  Every piece moves like a rook in chess: any number of empty squares
    along a row or a column. It cannot jump over pieces.
3.  Captures: a piece other than the king is captured when the enemy closes
    it in between two of their pieces on opposite sides, along a row or a
    column (not diagonally), or between one of their pieces and a hostile
    square. Only the moving side captures, so a piece may move in between
    two enemies safely. One move can capture several pieces. The king takes
    part in captures.
4.  Restricted squares: only the king may stop on the throne and the four
    corners. The king may go back onto the throne, and any piece may pass
    over the empty throne.
5.  Hostile squares: the corners are hostile to every piece. The throne is
    always hostile to the attackers, and hostile to the defenders only when
    it is empty. The edge of the board is not hostile.
6.  Shield wall: a row of two or more pieces along the edge, each with an
    enemy piece directly in front of it, is captured whole when the enemy
    closes both ends of the row. A corner may close one end. The king may
    help close a shield wall, as one of the pieces in front or at an end; a
    king inside an attacked row is not captured with it.
7.  The king is captured when attackers stand on all four sides of him. Next
    to the throne, three attackers on the other three sides are enough. On
    the edge he cannot be captured.
8.  The defenders win when the king reaches a corner, or when he builds an
    exit fort: he stands on the edge, is able to move, and the attackers can
    never break the fort.
9.  The attackers win when they capture the king, or when they close an
    unbroken ring round the king and all his remaining defenders.
10. Perpetual repetition is forbidden: it loses for the defenders.
11. A side that cannot move loses.

Our choices:

- “Perpetual repetition” is read as the same position, with the same side to
  move, arising for the third time. The defenders then lose, whichever side
  made the repeating move.
- A shield wall is captured only when the moving piece closes one end of it,
  standing on the edge.
- Whether a fort can never be broken is judged this way: the squares the
  king can reach hold no attacker and no attacker touches them, and every
  defender in the wall has, along its row and along its column, a side that
  is the board’s edge, the fort’s inside or another wall piece, so that no
  enemy can close it in.
- A ring counts when no defender has a path to any edge square through empty
  squares and other defenders.
- The rules call a game a draw when it cannot be ended, for example when
  both sides have too few pieces. The program does not judge that: such a
  game ends by repetition, which here loses for the defenders.

Fetlar 11x11
------------

The Fetlar Hnefatafl Panel drew up the Fetlar rules in 2007; they are
followed as Nielsen’s site gives them (Nielsen and Bartley 2026c). Fetlar is
in Shetland, where the World Quickplay Hnefatafl Championships are held each
summer; the Fetlar rules were for some time the international standard
before Copenhagen built on them (Wikipedia 2026). Board, setup, moves,
captures, restricted and hostile squares, the king’s capture and the ring
are as in Copenhagen. The differences:

1.  There is no shield wall and no exit fort. The king’s side wins only by
    reaching a corner.
2.  On the edge the king can still be captured if he is the last piece of
    his side and is surrounded on every side.
3.  Repetitions are allowed; a game that cannot be ended, because of
    repetitions or because both sides have too few pieces, is a draw.

Our choice: a game is drawn when a position, with the same side to move,
arises for the third time. As in Copenhagen, the program does not otherwise
judge that a game cannot be ended.

Brandubh 7x7
------------

The Irish game, in the World Tafl Federation’s official rules of 2020: an
Irish king, the branán, and his four princes against eight Viking invaders
(World Tafl Federation 2020). Moves, the throne and the ring work as in
Copenhagen. The differences:

1.  The corners are the four Irish provinces. The king escapes to any of
    them. They are hostile to both sides, the king included.
2.  The throne is never hostile to the king, always hostile to the
    attackers, and hostile to the defenders only when the king is not on it.
3.  The king is captured like any other piece, between two attackers or an
    attacker and a hostile square. Only on his throne do the attackers need
    all four sides of him. He takes part in captures.
4.  A ring round the king and all his defenders wins for the attackers only
    if it does not use the edge of the board.
5.  A situation repeated for the third time in a row loses for the player
    who chose to repeat it. A player who cannot move loses.
6.  A full match is two games, the players changing sides. The winner has
    won both, or has won his game in the fewest moves.

Our choices:

- Any third occurrence of a position, with the same side to move, counts,
  not only three in a row; the side whose move made it loses.
- The program plays single games, not the two-game match.

Tablut 9x9 (Linnaeus)
---------------------

Tablut as Linnaeus recorded it, in today’s reading of his diary (Ashton
2007; Cartier 2011). The program plays it by the historical rules on
Nielsen’s site, which give the king a capture by two and escape to the edge
(Nielsen and Bartley 2026a). Moves and captures are as in Copenhagen; the
defenders are the Swedes, the attackers the Muscovites. The differences:

1.  There are no restricted corners. The only special square is the throne,
    the konakis: only the king may stop there, and any piece may pass over
    it when it is empty. It is always hostile to the attackers and hostile
    to the defenders when empty.
2.  The king is captured like any other piece, between two attackers, except
    on and next to the throne. On the throne the attackers must stand on all
    four sides of him; next to it, on the other three.
3.  The Swedes win when the king reaches any square on the edge.
4.  The Muscovites win by capturing the king, or by closing a ring round him
    and all the Swedes.
5.  Perpetual repetition loses for the Swedes. A side that cannot move
    loses.

Linnaeus himself does not say who moves first, and reconstructions differ:
Ashton lets the Swedes start, Cartier the Muscovites (Ashton 2007, 3;
Cartier 2011, 6); the historical rules followed here give the first move to
the attackers (Nielsen and Bartley 2026a). They differ on the edge too:
Ashton reads the Muscovites’ embroidered starting squares as citadels the
king may not escape to, Cartier and the rules followed here make every edge
square a way out (Ashton 2007, 2; Cartier 2011, 6). Linnaeus’ law that no
one may enter the castle is read on Nielsen’s site as allowing the king back
onto it and others over it when empty; Wikipedia notes that Tablut is played
both ways (Wikipedia 2026).

Our choice: as in Copenhagen, the repetition rule is read as the third
occurrence of a position, which then loses for the Swedes.

Tablut 9x9 (Bell 1960)
----------------------

R. C. Bell’s Tablut, from Board and Table Games from Many Civilizations,
first published in 1960 and read here in the second edition (1969, 1:77-79).
Bell takes his account from Linnaeus’ diary in the 1811 translation (Bell
1969), and his rule for the king is that translation’s: four to capture him.

1.  Board and setup as in the other Tablut; only the Swedish king may occupy
    the central square, the konakis, or throne.
2.  All pieces move like the rook in chess.
3.  A piece is captured when the enemy occupies both squares next to it in a
    row or column. A piece may move safely between two enemies.
4.  The king is captured when enemy pieces stand on all four squares round
    him, or on three with the throne on the fourth. Then the Muscovites win.
5.  The Swedes win when the king reaches any square on the edge.
6.  When the king has a clear way to the edge, his player must warn Raichi!;
    with two, Tuichi!, which is checkmate, since both cannot be blocked in
    one move. The program calls these out.

The 1811 text spells the second call tuichu (Linnaeus 1811, 2:57); Bell
writes Tuichi, and so does the program.

Our choices, where Bell is silent:

- The Muscovites move first.
- Any piece may pass over the empty throne.
- The king takes part in captures.
- The empty throne is hostile, as in the other rule sets: always to the
  Muscovites, to the Swedes when empty. Bell’s text names only enemy pieces
  as capturing; the program also lets the throne help.
- There is no ring rule. A side that cannot move loses.
- A position arising for the third time, with the same side to move, is a
  draw.

Played this way the game favours the Swedes: Ashton says games played with a
king taken only by four are greatly unbalanced towards the defenders (Ashton
2007, 1).

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start hnefatafl, choose a rule set, whether you play against the computer
(easy, normal or hard) or a person at the same keyboard, and which side you
take.

  Key              
  ---------------- ----------------------------------------------
  Arrows           Move the cursor
  Enter or Space   Pick up a piece, then put it on a lit square
  Mouse            Click a piece, then a lit square
  Esc              Put the piece down again
  F1 or ?          These rules
  M                Back to the menu
  Q                Quit

After a game, Enter or Space starts another. hnefatafl --demo lets the
computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a game exactly.
