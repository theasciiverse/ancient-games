# Alquerque and Fanorona

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Played         | Alquerque: Spain and the Mediterranean, written down in 1283. Fanorona: Madagascar, described from 1878 |
| Players        | Two                                                   |
| Kind           | War games on lined boards: take every man of the other side |
| Program        | `alquerque`                                           |
| Rule sets      | Alquerque after Alfonso X; after Bell; Fanorona today; after Montgomery (1886); the vela; Fanoron-dimy |

::: brief
Two games on boards of points joined by lines: along every row and
column, and along the diagonals through every other point. A man moves
one step along a line to an empty point. In alquerque a man takes as
in draughts, leaping over an enemy man next to it to the empty point
beyond, and may leap on. In fanorona a man takes by moving towards an
enemy man (approach) or away from one next to it (withdrawal), and the
whole unbroken line of enemy men in that direction goes; it may take
again in a new direction. Whoever takes every man of the other side,
or leaves him no move, wins. The rules in short follow; the full rules,
the sources and the history are in RULES.txt and the rulebook.
:::

## What survives

**Alquerque.** The first rules of the game are in the *Book of Games*
that Alfonso X of Castile had made in Seville in 1283. Its sixth part,
the "Libro del alquerque", gives five games on three boards: Three,
Nine and Twelve Men's Morris (the morris chapter of this book), a hunt
called *cercar la liebre*, "corner the hare", and *alquerque de doze*,
alquerque of twelve, the leaping game of this chapter
[@golladay2007, 592]. "Alquerque" there is the name of the whole
family, and comes from the Arabic *al-qirq* [@golladay2007, 592 n.
990]. Alfonso puts it between chess and the dice and tables games,
since it is played by skill like chess, its men stand on marked places
like the men of tables, and dice decide who begins [@golladay2007,
624].

![Men playing alquerque de doze, from Alfonso X's *Libro de los juegos*,
1283. Real Biblioteca del Monasterio de San Lorenzo de El Escorial,
MS T-I-6. Public domain.](images/alquerque-libro-de-los-juegos.jpg){width=65%}

How old the game is, nobody can say. R. C. Bell gives an unfinished
alquerque board cut into the roof of the temple at Kurna in Egypt,
"built about 1400 B.C." [@bell1969, 47], after Parker's *Ancient
Ceylon*. Parker prints the Kurna figures but never calls one of them an
alquerque board, and none matches Bell's drawing [@parker1909,
643-644]; Friedrich Berger finds many of the Kurna figures to be Coptic
crosses, and says they "cannot be dated" [@berger2004, 15]. Bell also
finds the game in the Arabic *Kitab al-Aghani* of the 10th century
[@bell1969, 47]; the *qirq* there is, as far as Berger's sources show,
the morris board [@berger2004, 16]. No dated alquerque board older
than Alfonso's book is known to the sources of this chapter.

**Fanorona** is the national game of Madagascar. W. Montgomery, a
missionary, found it in 1886 played "everywhere, in-doors and out of
doors, in the town and in the country, and by all classes", its lines
scratched on rocks, on market sheds and on the paving outside the
palace [@montgomery1886, 148-149]; he wrote down the rules, a whole
game "of average native skill" and a game of the vela, the handicap
game played after a defeat [@montgomery1886, 152-156]. The Malagasy
royal histories that Father Callet gathered (second edition 1878) tell
the same: the setup, the board of nine and the board of five, the vela
[@callet1878, 142-143]. Both tell of a prince, Andriantompokoindrindra,
who would not leave a game to help his father in battle and lost the
kingdom; hence the proverb "Three against five, and toss away the
kingdom" [@montgomery1886, 151]. Montgomery dates the game's invention,
by a family tradition, "perhaps some two hundred years ago"
[@montgomery1886, 149]; the date of "about 1680" in popular books rests
on that guess. Fanorona's board is two alquerque boards side by side,
and its men take in a way found nowhere else.

In 2008 Maarten Schadd and four colleagues proved that fanorona, played
perfectly, is a draw [@schadd2008, 13].

Points are named by column, a letter from the left, and row, a number
from the bottom, White's side. The diagonals run through every point
whose column and row are both odd or both even (a1, b2, c1, the
corners and the centre); Montgomery draws the fanorona board as eight
squares of four "with the diagonal lines in each of the eight"
[@montgomery1886, 151], and Alfonso's board is the same pattern on five
by five [@golladay2007, 597].

## Alquerque de doze, after Alfonso X

::: brief
- 5 by 5 points, 12 men each; only the centre c3 is empty. White begins
  (Alfonso throws dice for it; our choice).
- A move: one step along a line, any way, to an empty point.
- Taking: leap along a line over an enemy man next to you onto the
  empty point beyond; take him; you may leap on over more, turning as
  you like, and stop when you like. Taking is not compulsory.
- Whoever loses all his men, or has no move, loses (no move: our
  choice). A third repetition, or 50 turns with nothing taken, draws
  (our choice).
:::

Alfonso: the board has "twenty-five places where the pieces may be
placed", twelve men a side "in a troop formation", and "there remains
one empty place in the center"; "the one that plays first has a
disadvantage because he is to play perforce in that empty space". A
man takes the man that moved next to it "by jumping over it from one
space to another as the straight lines on the board go", "and over as
many pieces as he should jump in this manner he will remove them all".
"The one who guards his pieces worse and loses them more quickly, is
defeated. And if both players know how to play it, frequently there
can be a tie" [@golladay2007, 624].

That is all. **Our choices**, where Alfonso is silent:

- **The middle row**: each side's two men on its own right, as Bell
  draws it [@bell1969, 47]; Alfonso's own diagram could not be read.
- **Moves** go any way along a line; nothing in the text limits them.
- **Taking is not compulsory**, and a man that has taken may stop:
  Alfonso says neither. The *huff*, losing a man that could have taken
  and did not, is first recorded in Covarrubias' dictionary of 1611:
  "in certain games of alquerque they call *soplar* taking the
  opponent's piece that could have taken and did not"
  [@covarrubias1611, s.v. soplar; our translation]. Sonja Musser
  Golladay, who translated the book, notes that the huff "is not
  explicit" in it [@golladay2007, 593].
- **No move** loses. **Ties**: a third repetition, or 50 turns with
  nothing taken.

## Alquerque, after Bell

::: brief
- As Alfonso's game, but taking is compulsory, and a man that has
  taken must take again while it can (the huff played as compulsion:
  our choice).
- No man steps backwards: only forwards, diagonally forwards or
  sideways. Leaps may go any way.
- No man may come back to a point it has stood on before.
- A man on the other side's back row moves only to take.
:::

Bell gives Alfonso's rules with "Suggested Additional Rules", since
"These rules from the Alfonso manuscript are not sufficient to play a
game" [@bell1969, 48]: compulsion with the huff ("If a piece can make a
capture it is forced to do so, otherwise it is huffed and is removed
from the board"), which he takes to be Alfonso's, and three rules of
his own: "No piece can move backwards; only forwards, diagonally
forwards, or sideways"; "No piece may return to a point that it has
been on before"; and "A piece reaching the opponent's back line is
unable to move except by making a capture by a short leap over an
enemy piece". He scores the game in points as well [@bell1969, 48].
Golladay calls his view that Alfonso's rules are not enough
"incorrect" [@golladay2007, 593].

**Our choices.** The program makes taking compulsory rather than
huffing: Bell does not say who takes the huffed man, or when. A man
must leap on while it can, as Bell's "taken in the same move" has it.
Bell's rule against coming back covers leaps too. Bell's points are not
kept. With these rules every game ends, since no man can go back.

## Fanorona, today's rules

::: brief
- 9 by 5 points, 22 men each; the middle row alternates, the centre e3
  empty. White begins.
- A move (paika): one step along a line to an empty point.
- Taking: a step that ends next to an enemy man, moving towards him,
  takes him and every enemy man straight behind him, up to the first
  empty point or man of yours (approach); a step away from an enemy man
  next to you takes him and the men behind him in the same way
  (withdrawal). If a step could do both, you choose.
- If you can take, you must. Then the same man may take again, as often
  as it can, but never twice running in the same direction, and never
  onto a point it has stood on this turn. You may stop at any time.
- Whoever takes every enemy man, or leaves him no move, wins. A third
  repetition draws; so do 50 turns with nothing taken (our choice).
:::

The game as Schadd and his colleagues give it, after Bell and the
Chauvicourts' rules of 1980 [@schadd2008, 3-6; @chauvicourt1980 as
cited in @schadd2008]: "Capturing moves are obliged and have to be
played, if possible, above non-capturing (paika) moves"; a man may go
on "as long as possible", and "the player may stop capturing after any
number of opponent pieces are captured"; it may not take "in the same
direction as the capturing move directly before", nor "arrive on the
same intersection twice during a capturing sequence" [@schadd2008,
4-6]. The game is a draw "if no player is able to win", in practice by
repetition; a player who cannot move loses, which they say no rules
they knew had settled [@schadd2008, 6]. Montgomery's account fits:
every capture in his sample game is the one these rules give
[@montgomery1886, 154].

## Fanorona, after Montgomery

::: brief
- As today's rules, but the very first turn of the game takes once
  only, and a man taking again may not go straight back along the line
  it came. It may take twice running in the same direction, and may
  come back to a point of this turn.
:::

Montgomery, 1886: "At the beginning of a game one move only is
permitted to the first side. After that side has moved once, any piece
that is moved is permitted to run amuck in the enemy's lines, and to go
on as long as he finds foes to capture, provided (a) that he does not
return immediately to any point he has just left, and (b) that he does
not take a foe behind him immediately after taking one in front of him,
nor one on his right hand immediately after taking on his left hand,
and *vice versa*. 'Dont eat at both ends, like a leech,' says the
Malagasy proverb" [@montgomery1886, 153].

Montgomery does not say that taking is compulsory. In his sample game
no player ever steps without taking when he could have taken
[@montgomery1886, 154], and Callet's words can be read the same way
[@callet1878, 142-143]. Ayres, who sold the game in London in 1889,
says it is not [@ayres1889, 44]. **Our choice**: compulsory, as the
sample game plays it. **Our reading** of (a) and (b): both forbid a
step straight back along the line of the last step; nothing else.

## Vela

::: brief
- The game after a defeat. Black, who won, may not take at all until he
  has lost seventeen men; then his last five play on as usual.
- White, who lost, moves first. Until then each of White's captures
  takes one man only, the nearest, and ends the turn; White must take
  when he can.
- Then everything is as in fanorona after Montgomery.
:::

"Should one of the players have been defeated, however, he is not
allowed to play on the same footing as before" [@montgomery1886,
154-155]. The game "is opened by the victor, who puts forward such of
his pieces as he chooses to surrender to his antagonist. These pieces
may only be taken singly, and the generous conqueror refrains from
taking any of his enemy's pieces, until he has parted with, one by one,
17 of his own pieces; then, with the remaining five, he begins his
campaign against the undiminished forces of his antagonist"
[@montgomery1886, 155]. The loser plays the vela until he wins one
[@montgomery1886, 155; @callet1878, 142-143]; in olden times, Montgomery
heard, the beaten player was forgiven "on condition of his kneeling
down before his conqueror and bleating like a sheep" [@montgomery1886,
155].

**Our reading.** In Montgomery's sample vela the loser moves first, and
each of his first seventeen captures takes one man only, the nearest,
with no capture after it [@montgomery1886, 155-156]; the program plays
it so. The computer, as the winner, offers its men; giving them is
strategy, not a rule.

## Fanoron-dimy

::: brief
- Fanorona on the board of five by five: 12 men each, the middle row
  Black, White, empty, Black, White. Today's rules.
:::

Callet names two fanoronas, "one of nine and one of five; the one of
five is a small, quick fanorona" [@callet1878, 142-143; our tentative
translation], and the dictionary of Abinal and Malzac has *andimy*,
the fanorona of five lines [@abinal1888, s.v. andimy]. No source read
for this chapter gives its setup or its rules: **the program's are by
analogy**, the middle row alternating as on the board of nine, and
today's rules. Schadd's team solved it too: a draw [@schadd2008, 13].

## Not offered

*Cercar la liebre*, Alfonso's hunt of one hare against ten to twelve
hunters on the alquerque board [@golladay2007, 625], and *fanoron-telo*
on three by three, which some books make a capture game and others a
game of three in a row.

## Playing it in the terminal

::: brief
- Arrows: move the cursor; Enter or Space: pick up a man, then put it
  on a lit point. A turn that takes again goes on from where the man
  stands: choose the next lit point, or press Esc (or point at the man)
  to stop, where stopping is allowed.
- If one step could take two lines, the men of both are lit: point at
  the line to take.
- The men a step would take are marked ✕ when the cursor is on where it
  goes. When the game is over, Enter plays again.
- M: menu; F1: this help; Q: quit.
:::

Start `alquerque`, choose the rules, whom to play against (the
computer, easy, normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and
which side you play.

| Key | |
|:--|:--|
| Arrows | Move the cursor |
| Enter or Space | Pick up a man; put it on a lit point; choose the line to take |
| Esc | Let go of the man, or end a turn of captures where it may end |
| Mouse | Click a man, then a point |
| F1 or ? | The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc closes them) |
| Enter or Space, after a game | Play again |
| M, or Esc after a game | Back to the menu |
| Q | Quit |

The computer searches ahead and judges a position by the men each side
has, the most it could take at once, and how freely each side can
move: easy one turn ahead and now and then any move at all, normal
three, hard as deep as it can in a second and a half.

`alquerque --demo` lets the computer play both sides; `--seed N`
repeats a game exactly.
