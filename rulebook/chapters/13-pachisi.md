# Pachisi and Chaupar

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Played         | India, from the 15th or 16th century; Akbar's court about 1590; and on, as Ludo |
| Players        | Four in two partnerships, partners opposite; in the program two players, each with a partnership |
| Kind           | Race game: four men a colour round the cross and home |
| Program        | `pachisi`                                             |
| Rule sets      | Pachisi after Tylor (1879); after Falkener (1892) and Bell; chaupar after Abu'l-Fazl (c. 1590) |

::: brief
A cross of four arms, each three columns by eight rows, round a square
in the middle. Four colours, four men each, one colour to an arm;
partners sit opposite and win together. A man comes out of the middle
down the middle row of its own arm, goes round the outside of the whole
cross against the sun, and back up its own middle row into the middle:
84 steps. Each throw moves one man its full value. A man landing on a
single enemy man sends him back to the middle, except on the castles
(the crossed squares). The partnership with all eight men home first
wins. Pachisi is thrown with six cowrie shells, chaupar with three long
dice. The rules in short follow; the full rules, the sources and the
history are in RULES.txt and the rulebook.
:::

## What survives

**How old.** Pachisi is often called the national game of India and
said to be thousands of years old: the dice game in which the Pandavas
lost their kingdom in the *Mahabharata*, or the game Shiva and Parvati
play in the cave temples of Ellora. Neither holds. The epic's game is
played with dice nuts and no board; "there is not the slightest reason
to ascribe the use of such a cloth to the epic period", as Heinrich
Lüders put it [@lueders1907, 14 n. 4; our translation]. The picture of
the gods at Ellora was retouched to show a cross-shaped board
[@schmidtmadsen2021, 39-40]. Jacob Schmidt-Madsen, who has gone through
the evidence, finds the first sure signs of the cross-shaped game in
Indian poems and paintings of the 15th and 16th centuries: "The
national game of India cannot be traced beyond the 15th century at the
earliest" [@schmidtmadsen2021, 35-37].

![*Shiva and Parvati Playing Chaupar*, folio from a Rasamanjari series by
Devidasa of Nurpur, Basohli, 1694-95. The Metropolitan Museum of Art,
New York, 57.185.2, CC0.](images/pachisi-shiva-parvati-met.jpg){width=75%}

**Akbar.** The first account of the rules is in the *Ain-i Akbari*, the
record of the emperor Akbar's court that Abu'l-Fazl wrote about 1590:
"From times of old, the people of Hindústán have been fond of this
game"; "Formerly many grandees took part in this game; there were often
as many as two hundred players, and no one was allowed to go home
before he had finished sixteen games, which in some cases lasted three
months. If any of them lost his patience and got restless, he had to
drink a cup of wine" [@blochmann1873, 303-304]. The story that Akbar
played on a board paved in a courtyard at Fatehpur Sikri with slave
girls for men is not in Abu'l-Fazl; the earliest form of it found for
this book is the translator Blochmann's own note to his plate, and
Rousselet tells it as "It is said ..." [@blochmann1873;
@rousselet1882, 302].

**The names.** In the Panjab "the game of chaupur occurs so frequently
in these legends, and its technicalities play so important a part in
them" that Richard Temple described it: with dice it is *phansa*, with
cowries *pachisi* [@temple1884, 243-245]. The name comes from the
highest throw: "the game is generally played with six cowries, making
the highest throw 25 ... hence it is termed Pucheesee (from puchees,
25)" [@herklots1832, liii]. The book follows Temple and
Schmidt-Madsen in calling the dice game chaupar and the cowrie game
pachisi [@schmidtmadsen2021, 33]. The first European picture of the
board is Thomas Hyde's in 1694; the man he asked "could not teach me
the way of playing" [@hyde1694, 69-70].

**The rules,** like the game, vary: "one native gives one description
of the game, and another another" [@falkener1892, 264]. The fullest
early rules are those Rajendralala Mitra of Calcutta gave Edward Tylor,
printed in 1879 [@tylor1879, 118-120]; Edward Falkener's of 1892 are
the ones R. C. Bell reprints, almost word for word [@falkener1892,
257-262; @bell1969, 9-12]. The English game of Ludo was taken from
pachisi in the 1880s [@schmidtmadsen2021, 34].

## The board

![The cross with Red's way numbered: out down the middle row (1 to 8),
round against the sun (9 to 76) and back up the middle row (77 to 83)
into the middle. Crosses: Tylor's castles; circles: Falkener's; both at
the end of each middle row.](figures/pachisi-board.svg){width=75%}

The board is a cloth, often embroidered; "zealous players often
carrying one rolled round in their turbans" [@tylor1879, 118]. All the
sources agree on the cross of four arms of three columns by eight rows,
and on the way round: out down one's own middle row, round the outside
rows "from right to left (i.e. contrary to the sun)", and back up the
middle row into the middle [@herklots1832, lii; @tylor1879, 119;
@falkener1892, 261]. The program numbers a man's way 1 to 84 as above,
from Falkener's numbered drawing [@falkener1892, 263].

There are twelve castles, "the twelve privileged squares, which are
marked with a cross" [@herklots1832, lii]: the outer end of each middle
row, and two on each arm's outer rows. Where those two stand, the
sources differ: Tylor's drawing puts them on the fourth square from the
middle [@tylor1879, 119], Falkener's and Bell's on the fourth from the
outer end [@falkener1892, 263; @bell1969, 10]. Culin found both on real
boards [@culin1898, 854 n. 1]. Each rule set keeps its own source's.

## Rules every set shares

::: brief
- Two partnerships: Red and Black against Yellow and Green; you play
  both colours of yours. Red throws first, then Yellow, Black, Green,
  round against the sun (our choice).
- Each throw moves one man its whole value; a throw is never split.
  Throws may be used in any order, on any men, and may be left unused
  (P leaves the rest).
- Landing on a lone enemy man outside a castle sends it back to the
  middle. Partners never take each other, and may share a square.
- Men pass over any men. Going home needs the exact number.
:::

**Our choices**, where the sources are silent: the order of play, round
against the sun like the men; men passing freely over others (no
source mentions blocking a way); and two players, each with a
partnership, as Falkener and Temple describe for two players
[@falkener1892, 264; @temple1884, 244], since the program's menu has
two sides.

## Pachisi, after Tylor

::: brief
- Six cowries; count the mouths up: none 6, one 10, two 2, three 3,
  four 4, five 25, six 12. A 6, 10, 12 or 25 throws again; then the
  throws are played.
- A 10 or a 25 also brings a starting 1: the only way a man enters
  (onto the first square), and the way from square 83 home.
- Two or more men of one colour hold their square: no enemy may land
  there. A castle with an enemy man on it is shut to you.
- Taking a man brings another throw.
- Rotten throws: three high throws (10, 12, 25) running are struck out,
  unless the next throw is a 10 or a 25.
- A colour with all its men home throws for its partner (our choice).
:::

The rules Rajendralala Mitra gave Tylor, "a more complete and
consistent set of rules" than earlier ones [@tylor1879, 118]. The
cowries count as Herklots and Shway Yoe also give them [@tylor1879,
119; @herklots1832, lii-liii; @shwayyoe1896, 375]:

```{.example file="pachisi/throws-tylor"}
```

- "The high throws 6, 10, 12, 25, entitle the player to a new throw ...
  but at the lower throws the lead passes to the other player."
- A 10 or a 25 brings "a starting 1 (*puá*) in addition to the ordinary
  score, by which 1 a-piece is put on the first square"; "the throws are
  useless till one player throws 10 or 25" [@tylor1879, 119-120].
- "A single man on a square is taken by an enemy's man moving on to
  that square, and the taken man being dead (*mard*) is put back in the
  home to start afresh"; "taking or cutting (*kátá*) a man gives the
  player a new throw"; "two or more men of one set on a square hold it
  safely, all which is as in our backgammon"; "the crossed squares or
  forts are places where a single man is in safety, and even blocks an
  enemy's man from moving there" [@tylor1879, 120].
- A man coming home "is called ripe (*pakká*)"; going home "a player
  cannot use a throw for which there are not sufficient squares left",
  and a man on the last square "can only be got off the board as it got
  on, by a throw of das or pachisi" [@tylor1879, 120 and n.].
- "If 25, 10, or 12 are thrown thrice running, they are called rotten
  (*pachá*) and destroy one another, but a new throw of 10 or 25 may
  restore them, and so with six consecutive throws, restored by a
  seventh" [@tylor1879, 120 n.].

```{.example file="pachisi/tylor-turn"}
```

**Our readings and choices.** Throws may be left unused (Tylor is
silent; Falkener allows it). The rotten throws: within a turn, the
third (or sixth) high throw running puts the last three in doubt; the
next throw, if a 10 or a 25, restores them, and otherwise they are
struck out, and if the turn ends on them they are lost:


A partner whose men are all home "can throw on his partner's behalf,
after getting a new starting 1" [@tylor1879, 120 n.]; the program lets
him throw for his partner at once. Left out: moving two men as a
couple (*jora*), which Tylor says good players do not recognise, and a
ripe man going round again [@tylor1879, 120 n.].

## Pachisi, after Falkener

::: brief
- Six cowries: none up 25, one 10, two 2, three 3, four 4, five 5, six
  6. A 25, 10 or 6 throws again; a 25 or a 10 brings a grace of 1.
- One man of each colour starts out in the middle; the others come out
  only with a grace. Once out, a man moves from the middle with any
  throw: 25 from the middle reaches the castle at the end of the next
  arm.
- Castles are shut to the enemy. Men may double on other squares at
  their own peril: one enemy man landing there takes them all.
- Taking a man brings another throw. A colour with all its men home
  simply waits.
:::

Falkener's rules, which Bell follows [@falkener1892, 259-262;
@bell1969, 9-11]. His cowries count differently from Tylor's, and a 25
is rare:

```{.example file="pachisi/throws-falkener"}
```

- "In commencing the game the first piece may be entered whatever throw
  is made, but the other pieces can enter only with a grace. So,
  likewise, a piece taken up can enter only with a grace"
  [@falkener1892, 261].
- The castles: "Any number of pieces of a player or of his partner are
  safe in these castles, and an enemy cannot enter"; "Pieces may double
  on other squares, but it is at their own peril ... they can be taken
  off by a single piece at one stroke" [@falkener1892, 260].
- "On taking a piece the player may throw again"; "A player may refuse
  to play when it comes to his turn, or he may throw and then refuse to
  take it" [@falkener1892, 261].
- Falkener also notes that the castles stand 25 apart, from the middle:
  0 to 25, 21 to 46, 59 to home [@falkener1892, 260]. The program's
  numbering bears him out.
- "Sometimes the player who is out first is permitted to give his
  throws to his partner, but this is not the game" [@falkener1892, 262];
  the program follows "the game".

**Bell's differences.** Bell drops the grace and lets men enter on a 6,
10 or 25 [@bell1969, 11]; but he keeps the exact finish, so a man on the
last square, needing a 1, could never get home. The program plays
Falkener's grace. **Our reading** of the grace: it lets a man out into
the middle, from where any throw moves it, as Falkener's counting of
the castles from the middle requires.

## Chaupar, after Abu'l-Fazl

::: brief
- Three long dice, each showing 1, 2, 5 or 6. Each die moves one man;
  no throwing again.
- The men start on the board: two on squares 6 and 7 of their middle
  row, two on 15 and 16 of the row on their right.
- A doublet may move two men standing together twice its value each
  (keeping them together); a triplet three. Otherwise dice are used
  one by one.
- Two or more men together cannot be taken, except by a pair moved
  together by a doublet. There are no castles.
- A man taken goes to the middle and comes back on a die of 1, onto the
  first square (our choice).
- A player with all his men home throws for his partner.
:::

Abu'l-Fazl describes the dice: "Four of the six sides of each dice are
greater than the remaining two, the four long sides being marked with
one, two, five, and six dots respectively". "Generally played by four
players, of whom two play against the other two. Each player has four
pieces"; he "puts two in the sixth and seventh spaces of the middle row
of the parallelogram before him, and the other two in the seventh and
eighth spaces of the right row. The left row remains empty". "As long
as a player keeps two of his pieces together, the adversary cannot
throw them out." "If a player throws a double six, he can move two
pieces over twelve spaces, provided the two pieces stand together on
one field ... A similar rule holds for double fives, &c." "If a player
has brought his four pieces into the central square, he throws, when
his turn comes, for his companion, to get him out too"
[@blochmann1873, 303-304].


**Our readings and choices.** Where the men start: Abu'l-Fazl does not
say from which end he counts; counting along the way the men go puts
them on 6, 7, 15 and 16, and Falkener's "6 and 7" agrees for the first
two [@falkener1892, 264]. Each die moves a man by itself, as Falkener's
doublets "given to a single piece" or "to two pieces" imply. A pair
moved together by a doublet can take a pair, as Falkener's Chausar
allows: "they cannot be taken except by doubles" [@falkener1892, 264].
The throw of six, five and one Abu'l-Fazl calls *khám*, with its own
rule, is left out, being obscure; so are the betting and the fines he
describes [@blochmann1873, 303-304]. A man taken comes back with a die
of 1, the *paun*, the entering throw of the cowrie game
[@temple1884, 245]; no source says how.

## The ornament

On a terminal larger than 100 by 30, the four corners beside the
program's cross carry a flower from a painting in the Metropolitan
Museum: Ambav's "Maharaja Sovan Singh playing pachisi", Udaipur, about
1868 [@met2019123]. The square at the centre of the painted board holds a
white flower on red: eight long pointed petals, eight short ones between
them. It is drawn in braille dots in a dim tone, never over a man, a
highlight or a number. No board earlier than the nineteenth century was
found, so this is not offered as the ornament of Akbar's time. O turns
it off and on, and `pachisi --plain` starts without it.

## Playing it in the terminal

::: brief
- Enter or Space: throw. Then arrows (or the mouse) to a lit man,
  Enter to pick it up, a lit square to put it down; the middle stands
  for men still to come out. P: leave the rest of the throws unused.
- The panel shows the throw as it fell and the throws still to use
  (1* a starting 1 or grace).
- When the game is over, Enter plays again.
- M: menu; F1: this help; Q: quit.
- O: the ornament off and on (the flower of a painted pachisi board,
  Udaipur, about 1868); `--plain` starts without it.
:::

Start `pachisi`, choose the rules, whom to play against (the computer,
easy, normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which
partnership you play.

| Key | |
|:--|:--|
| Enter or Space | Throw; pick up a man; put it on a lit square |
| Arrows | Move the cursor round the cross |
| Esc | Let go of the man |
| P | Leave the rest of the throws unused |
| Mouse | Click to throw, to pick up and to put down |
| F1 or ? | The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc closes them) |
| Enter or Space, after a game | Play again |
| M, or Esc after a game | Back to the menu |
| O | The ornament off and on |
| Q | Quit |

The computer plans the use of all its throws at once, trying every
order and every man: easy moves forward and takes when it can, and now
and then plays any move; normal judges where the men end by the race,
the men in safety and the enemies behind them; hard reckons how likely
each exposed man is to be hit, and plays out the next enemy turn from
its best few plans. The throws decide much.

`pachisi --demo` lets the computer play both sides; `--seed N` repeats
a game exactly; `--plain` leaves out the ornament.
