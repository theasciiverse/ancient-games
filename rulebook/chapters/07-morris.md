# Morris

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Played         | Rome by the first century AD; all Europe in the Middle Ages; today worldwide |
| Players        | Two                                                   |
| Kind           | Game of position: three in a row                      |
| Program        | `morris`                                              |
| Rule sets      | Nine Men's Morris, today's rules; Nine Men's Morris after Bell; Morabaraba; Six Men's Morris after Bell; Three Men's Morris after Bell; Three Men's Morris, placing only |

::: brief
Two players enter their men one at a time on the points of a board of
lines, then move them along the lines. Three men of one colour in a row
along a line make a mill. In Three Men's Morris a mill wins; in the
larger games each mill takes one of the other side's men, and a side
left with two men, or with no move, has lost. The rules of each form in
short follow; the full rules, their sources and the history are in
RULES.txt and the rulebook.
:::

## What survives

**The game.** The morris games are played on a board of points joined
by lines. Each player enters men of his colour on the points, one at a
time, and in most forms then moves them along the lines. Three men of
one colour on one line make a row, a *mill*. In the smallest game a
mill wins; in the larger ones it lets its maker take one of the other
side's men. R. C. Bell puts them first among his "games of position"
[@bell1969, 91-95].

![A Roman brick with a morris board scratched into its face, bone
counters laid on it; lent by the Römisch-Germanisches Museum, Cologne,
to the exhibition *Bretter, die die Welt bedeuten* (2018). Photograph:
Raimond Spekking, CC BY-SA 4.0.](images/morris-roman-brick-cologne.jpg){height=8cm}

The names come from the pieces. Friedrich Berger traces *merels*,
French *marelle* and *mérelles*, to the Latin *marellus*, a little man
or game piece, and the German *Mühle*, the mill, to *mîle*, from the
Latin *miles*, the foot soldier, used for the game around 1215 [@berger2004, 16].
"Morris" is the English name, and Joseph Strutt guessed at its reason
in 1801: "from the moving these men backwards or forwards, as though
they were dancing a morris" [@strutt1845].

**Ovid.** The first words about any rule are Roman, and they are about
the game of three men a side. Ovid, in his *Art of Love*, lists the
games a young woman should know:

> | *Parva tabella capit ternos utrimque lapillos,*
> | *in qua vicisse est continuasse suos.*
>
> A smaller board presents three stones each on either side, where the
> winner will have made his line up together.

Ovid's lines are from book III, 365-366 [@ovid-ars], here and below in
A. S. Kline's translation. He repeats them almost word for word in the
*Tristia*, the poems of his exile: "how a small board's set with three
'stones' a side, and winning rests in keeping them together"
[@ovid-tristia, book II].
Nothing in either passage is about the larger board.

**The oldest boards.** Bell gives two very early dates, and both rest
on H. Parker's *Ancient Ceylon* of 1909 [@bell1969, 92-93; @parker1909, 577-580]:

- Boards cut into the roofing slabs of the temple at Kurna (Qurna) in
  Egypt, which Bell dates to about 1400 BC, the time the temple was
  built: a board for Nine Men's Morris and, he says, a board for the
  game of three. Parker found three of the designs partly cut away
  where the slabs were trimmed, so they were cut before the stones were
  laid, and took the others to be as old. Berger reports that Parker
  lists 34 figures from Kurna in all, many of them Coptic crosses, one
  of them inside a merels board, and that Claude Wagneur concluded from
  this that the figures are Coptic. "Certainly they cannot be dated,"
  Berger writes [@berger2004, 15].
- Two boards on the great stairway at Mihintale in Sri Lanka, which Bell
  dates to the reign of Mahadathika Maha-Naga, AD 9 to 21. Parker
  reports that date from tradition; his own estimate is that the steps
  were "already built by 245 A.D." and most probably date to the second
  century or the beginning of the first [@parker1909, 577-578]. Berger
  notes that no source for Bell's dates is given [@berger2004, 15].

So neither is a safe date. The oldest dated boards Berger knows are
Roman: one on a tile from the last level of the town of Enserune in
southern France, about 100 BC to AD 40, and one cut into a tile of the
Thirtieth Legion at Xanten in Germany before it was fired, so after
AD 196. Many more are scratched into Roman buildings, but cannot be
dated, since they may have been drawn later [@berger2004, 15-16].

**The Vikings.** In the Gokstad ship burial in Norway, opened in 1880,
lay part of an oak gaming board, cut on one side for Nine Men's Morris
and on the other for a game that may have been hnefatafl
[@bell1969, 93-94; @vikingship-morris]. The burial dates from about
the year 900.

**The Middle Ages.** By the thirteenth century the game was played from
Castile to Novgorod. The *Libro de los juegos* that Alfonso X of Castile
had made in 1283 gives the first written rules of morris anywhere, for
nine men with dice and without, and for three (see below)
[@golladay2007, 623-627]. Bell writes that the game "reached its zenith
in the fourteenth century", that the codices of the North Italian
academies collect morris problems as they collect chess problems, and
that boards for it were cut into the cloister seats of Norwich,
Canterbury, Gloucester and Salisbury cathedrals and Westminster Abbey
[@bell1969, 92-94]. From the fourteenth century on, boxes were made
with chess on one side of the lid, Nine Men's Morris on the other and
backgammon inside [@bell1969, 95].

In England "nine men's morris" was also cut into the turf. When Titania
describes the ruined summer in *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, "The nine
men's morris is filled up with mud" [@shakespeare-mnd]. Strutt, in
1801, still saw shepherds play it that way, the lines cut in the ground
and a hole for every point [@strutt1845].

**The board with diagonals.** The board of Nine Men's Morris with its
corners joined is the board of Twelve Men's Morris. Berger, after H. J.
R. Murray, finds it first in Arabic sources: Murray cites the *Kitab
al-Aghani*, compiled between 918 and 967, for a house in Mecca kept with
sets of chess, nard and *qirq*, the Arabic name for the game
[@berger2004, 16, citing Murray]. In southern Africa the game on this
board, Morabaraba, is played to this day.

**What is not known.** Nothing written before Alfonso X gives the rules
of any morris game, and nothing in Ovid goes beyond three in a row. The
rules below come from three kinds of source: Bell's short accounts
[@bell1969, 91-94], the rules clubs and federations play by now
[@wmd2008; @mssa-morabaraba], and Alfonso's book for the game of three
[@golladay2007]. Where they differ, the program offers each.

## The boards

![The board of Nine Men's Morris: three squares, one inside another,
joined at the middles of their sides. Twenty-four points, sixteen lines
of three.](figures/morris-nine.svg){width=55%}

This book names the points as players of Nine Men's Morris write them:
columns a to g from left to right, rows 1 to 7 from bottom to top, so
that a1 is the bottom left corner and g7 the top right. The smaller
boards are named the same way. The program shows no names but uses them
in its messages.

A mill is three men of one colour on one line of three points. On the
board of nine there are sixteen: four along the sides of each square
and four across. A man moves along a line to the next point.

## Nine Men's Morris, today's rules

::: brief
- 9 men each. White begins. In turn, each enters a man on any empty
  point until all 18 are in; then each moves one man a turn along a
  line to the next empty point.
- Three of your men on one line make a mill: take one of the other
  side's men, but not one in a mill, unless all of them are in mills.
  Two mills at once still take one man. A mill opened and closed again
  takes again.
- Down to three men, a side flies: it moves a man to any empty point.
- A side left with two men, or with no move, loses.
- The same position with the same side to move a third time: a draw.
:::

The game as clubs and tournaments play it. The rules here are those of
the World Mill Association's leaflet of 2008 [@wmd2008, 6] and of Ralph
Gasser's paper of 1996, which solved the game: with perfect play from
both sides, it is a draw [@gasser1996, 101-103].

1. Each player has nine men. The board is empty at the start. White
   begins (Gasser).
2. In turn, each enters one man on any empty point, until all are in.
3. Then each in turn moves one of his men along a line to the next
   point, if it is empty.
4. A player who makes a mill, by entering or by moving, takes one of
   his opponent's men off the board. "Any piece may be taken, except
   those being in a closed Mühle" [@wmd2008, 6]. A mill may be opened
   by moving a man out of it and closed again by moving it back; each
   time it is closed it takes a man.
5. "A player who has only 3 pieces left, may leap, in other words he
   may place his pieces on any desired point" [@wmd2008, 6].
6. "If a player has only 2 pieces left, he has lost the game." So has a
   player who cannot move [@wmd2008, 6].

![The program playing today's rules, the computer against itself.
](screens/morris/nine.svg){width=100%}

Two points, Gasser writes, "are subject to debate among Nine Men's
Morris enthusiasts" [@gasser1996, 103]: whether two mills made at once
take one man or two, and whether a mill may take anything when all the
other side's men stand in mills. The World Mill Association counts both
as regional deviations it does not accept, without saying what its own
rule is [@wmd-rules-page]. Gasser's program let two mills take one man,
and let a mill take any man when all were in mills; it seemed unlikely
to him that either choice changes the value of the game. Rainer
Rosenberger's handbook says the association has since dropped its ban
on taking from a mill [@rosenberger2016, 32].

Our choices:

- Two mills made by one move take one man, as Gasser.
- When every man of the other side stands in a mill, a mill may take
  any of them, as Gasser.
- The same position with the same side to move, a third time, is a
  draw. Gasser counts a game drawn as soon as a position comes back; the
  leaflet does not say.

## Nine Men's Morris after Bell

::: brief
- As today's rules, with these differences:
- No flying: a side down to three still moves along the lines.
- A mill never takes a man that stands in a mill; if every one does, it
  takes nothing.
- A side left with two men, or with no move, loses. A side whose only
  men stand in a mill must still move, even out of it.
:::

Bell's account [@bell1969, 93]:

1. Each player has nine men, and they "enter them alternately one at a
   time, on any vacant point".
2. "Each time a player forms a row or mill of three pieces along a line
   he removes one of his opponent's pieces from the board, but not one
   which is in a mill."
3. When all are entered, each in turn moves a man "to an adjacent vacant
   point along a line".
4. "A player blocking all his opponent's men so that they cannot move,
   or reducing him to two pieces, wins the game."
5. "A mill can be made and broken any number of times; an enemy piece
   being captured each time the mill is made. If a player only has
   three pieces in a mill left on the board and it is his turn to move,
   he must do so even if this means losing a piece and the game at his
   opponent's next move."

The last rule makes plain that Bell's game has no flying: a player with
three men moves them along the lines like any other. Strutt's account of
1801 has none either, and ends the game only when one side "takes off
all his antagonist's pieces" [@strutt1845]; the program does not offer
that ending.

Our choices:

- Who begins: White, as today. Bell does not say.
- When every man of the other side stands in a mill, a mill takes
  nothing. Bell's words, "not one which is in a mill", allow no
  exception.
- Two mills made by one move take one man. Bell does not say.
- A third repetition is a draw, as above.

## Morabaraba, Mind Sports South Africa

::: brief
- 12 cows (men) each on the board with diagonals: the corners of the
  three squares are joined, and mills run along the diagonals too.
  Cows move along any line, diagonals included.
- A mill shoots (takes) one cow, not one in a mill while there is any
  other; two mills at once still shoot one.
- A cow that left a mill to make a new one may not go straight back on
  your next move if that makes a mill again.
- Down to three cows, a side flies. Two cows left, or no move: lost.
- The board filled with no cow shot: a draw. A side down to three and no
  cow shot in ten moves each: a draw.
:::

![The board of twelve: the board of nine with its corners joined along
the diagonals. Twenty lines of three.](figures/morris-twelve.svg){width=55%}

With twelve men each, Mind Sports South Africa's Generally Accepted
Rules [@mssa-morabaraba]:

1. "Each player starts with 12 tokens called 'cows'." They are placed in
   turn on empty points; mills count "vertically, diagonally, or
   horizontally".
2. A mill shoots one of the opponent's cows. "A player can not shoot an
   opponent's 'three-in-a-row' cows if there are other cows on the board
   left to shoot." "Only one of the opponent's cows may be shot at a
   time, even if two (or more) lines of three-in-a-row are achieved with
   one move."
3. When all are placed, a cow moves to an empty point "immediately
   linked and adjacent". Mills may be broken and made again, and each
   time a mill is made again a cow is shot. But "if a player breaks a
   line to immediately create a new line of three cows, such player may
   not move such cow back to the original junction in his/her next
   move, if it creates a new line."
4. "When a player has lost all but three cows", his cows fly to any
   empty point.
5. The game is won when the opponent cannot move, or has lost all but
   two cows. "When a player is down to three cows, and neither player is
   able to shoot an opponent's cow within ten moves, the game is a
   draw."

Twenty-four cows fill the twenty-four points. If none is shot while
placing, nobody can move. The rules above do not say what happens then.
Rosenberger gives the game as a draw [@rosenberger2016, 43]; Gábor
Gévay and Gábor Danner, who solved the game on this board with twelve
men each under Gasser's rules (with perfect play White wins), note that
there is no consensus on such a rule, and that without it the full board
is a loss for White, the side to move [@gevay2016].

Our choices:

- A board filled while placing is a draw, as Rosenberger.
- The ten moves are ten of each side, twenty in all, counted from the
  moment a side is down to three cows and started again at every shot.
- Who begins: White.
- A third repetition is a draw, as above.

Berger reports, after Murray, an older rule for this board: men move
along the diagonals but make no mill there [@berger2004, 15, citing
Murray]. The program does not offer it.

## Six Men's Morris after Bell

::: brief
- 6 men each on a board of two squares (16 points), their sides'
  middles joined. Mills only along the sides of the squares.
- Enter all men in turn, then move along the lines, as in Nine Men's
  Morris. No flying.
- A mill takes any one man of the other side, in a mill or not.
- A side left with two men, or with no move, loses.
:::

![The board of Six Men's Morris: two squares, joined at the middles of
their sides. Sixteen points, eight lines of
three.](figures/morris-six.svg){width=45%}

"Six Men's Morris was popular in Italy, France, and England during the
Middle Ages but was obsolete by 1600" [@bell1969, 92]:

1. "Each player had six pieces and they were entered alternately, one
   at a time; each player trying to form a row along one of the sides
   of either square."
2. "If a player succeeded in this he removed any one of his opponent's
   pieces."
3. "When all the pieces had been entered the game continued by
   alternate moves of a piece along a line to an adjacent empty point."
4. "When a player was reduced to two men the game was over."

Our choices:

- "Any one" of the opponent's men: a mill takes a man in a mill as
  readily as any other.
- A player who cannot move loses, as in Bell's Nine Men's Morris. Bell
  does not say.
- White begins; two mills at once take one man; a third repetition is a
  draw.

## Three Men's Morris after Bell

::: brief
- 3 men each on 9 points: a square of three by three, joined along the
  rows, the columns and both diagonals. White begins.
- Enter the men in turn on empty points, then move one a turn along a
  line to the next empty point.
- The first to have his three men in a row along a line wins. Nothing
  is taken.
- The same position a third time: a draw. With perfect play the side
  that begins wins.
:::

![The board of Three Men's Morris, with its diagonals, as Bell draws the
board he connects with Kurna (his fig. 78). Eight lines of
three.](figures/morris-three.svg){width=35%}

Bell's second form of the game [@bell1969, 92]:

1. "Each player had three men of his own colour and entered them in
   turn on any vacant point on the board,"
2. "and then the game continued with alternate moves until one player
   succeeded in placing his three men in a straight line."
3. "As the first player could always force a win by correct play, dice
   may have been used to decide the advantage of first entry."

Alfonso X's *alquerque de tres*, of 1283, is played on the same board
with three men each, six in all: "he who plays first wins if he should
know how to play it well. And the play of it is this: he who should
more quickly place his pieces in a row wins" [@golladay2007, 626]. The
first player, the book goes on, should enter his first man in the
middle, and his second so that the other must block it; then he must
block in turn, "and if in this way he should have placed them so that
wherever the other player puts his remaining piece he loses"
[@golladay2007, 626-627].

Alfonso does not say whether the men move once they are in. Sonja
Musser Golladay, his translator, reads the game as placing only, like
noughts and crosses [@golladay2007, 607-608, 626]. But a game of
placing only, three men each, is a draw with correct play: the
program's tests work out every position of it, and of Bell's game, from
the end back. Alfonso's first player who "wins if he should know how to
play it well" fits Bell's game, in which the men move: there the first
player does win, if he plays well. The program therefore offers Bell's
game, and takes it to be Alfonso's too.

Our choices:

- A man moves one step along a line, diagonals included. Bell says only
  "alternate moves".
- White begins, and may enter his first man anywhere, the middle
  included; none of our sources forbids it.
- A third repetition is a draw.

## Three Men's Morris, placing only

::: brief
- 4 counters each on 9 points: a square of three by three, joined along
  the rows and columns, without diagonals. White begins.
- In turn, each places a counter on an empty point. Counters never
  move.
- Three in a row along a row or a column wins. All eight placed and no
  row: a draw.
:::

![The board of Bell's first form: a square of three by three without
diagonals (his fig. 77). Six lines of
three.](figures/morris-three-plain.svg){width=35%}

Bell's first form [@bell1969, 91-92]: "The board contains nine points
and each player has four counters of his own colour. The players place
one counter alternately on a point, and if one player can place three
in a straight line he wins the game." His figure has no diagonals. It
is noughts and crosses with counters, and as in noughts and crosses
correct play from both sides draws.

Our choices: Bell says nothing of moving the counters, so they are only
placed, and when all eight are down without a row the game is drawn.
White begins.

## Other variants

None of these is in the program.

**Nine Men's Morris with dice.** Alfonso X's book also has a game of
nine men with three dice for the placing. As Golladay translates it:
"the first to play rolls the dice first, if the pips of them should say
6-5-4, 3-3-6, 5-2-2, or 1-1-4, for whichever of these rolls that he may
throw, he will place three pieces in a row and he will remove each time
one of the pieces of the other player. And if he rolls so that he makes
two mills he will remove two pieces" [@golladay2007, 625-626]. Bell describes the same throws but
a different rule: they let the thrower take a man out of an enemy mill
as well as enter one, any other throw only enters a man, and the dice
are put away once all are entered [@bell1969, 94]. Golladay calls
Bell's account incorrect [@golladay2007, 606]. What exactly the lucky
throws do is not clear enough from the text to play.

**Alquerque de doze.** The game of twelve in Alfonso's book is not
Twelve Men's Morris but the game of alquerque: twelve men each on a
board of twenty-five points, capturing by jumping, as in draughts
[@golladay2007, 623-624].

## Playing it in the terminal

::: brief
- Arrows: move the cursor; Enter or Space: enter a man on an empty
  point, or pick up a man and put it down on a lit point; Esc: let go.
  Or click.
- After a mill, the men it may take are lit red: choose one.
- When the game is over, Enter plays again.
- M: menu; F1: this help; Q: quit.
:::

Start `morris`, choose a rule set, whom to play against (the computer,
easy, normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side
you play.

| Key | |
|:--|:--|
| Arrows | Move the cursor |
| Enter or Space | Enter a man; pick up a man, then put it down on a lit point; choose the man a mill takes |
| Esc | Let go of the man, or take back a move whose mill has not yet taken |
| Mouse | Click a point |
| F1 or ? | The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc closes them) |
| Enter or Space, after a game | Play again |
| M, or Esc after a game | Back to the menu |
| Q | Quit |

The side panel shows how many men each side has on the board and still
to enter. When a man is picked up, the points it can go to are lit;
after a mill, the men it may take turn red.

The computer plays at three strengths. Easy searches two moves ahead
and now and then plays any move at all; normal searches four moves deep;
hard searches as deep as it can in a second and a half. All three keep
away from positions the game has already seen. Three Men's Morris is
small enough to solve when the game starts, a few thousand positions,
and hard plays both its forms perfectly: as the first player in the
moving game it always wins.

`morris --demo` lets the computer play both sides; `--seed N` repeats a
game exactly.
