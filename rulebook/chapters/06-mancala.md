# Mancala

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Played         | Africa, the Middle East, South and Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, the Baltic; the earliest likely boards about the 6th century AD |
| Players        | Two                                                   |
| Kind           | Sowing game: count and capture                        |
| Program        | `mancala`                                             |
| Rule sets      | Oware (Abapa), Wari (Bell), Mankal'ah of the ignorant and of the wise (Lane), Kalah (Champion), Congkak (Hellier), Pallanguli (Durai), Toguz Kumalak, Bohnenspiel |

## What survives

Mancala is not one game but a family of hundreds. All of them are played
with seeds, shells or pebbles in rows of pits: a player lifts all the
seeds of one pit and sows them one by one into the pits that follow,
and captures by where the last seed falls. Almost everything after that
differs from one place to the next, and this chapter offers the games of
several regions, each with its own rules.

**The name.** Thomas Hyde, the Oxford orientalist, wrote the first
European account of the game in 1694. He gives the name in Arabic,
*manqala*, "commonly Mángala", and explains it: it is so called "because
they move the pebbles from place to place", and so he calls it in Latin
the game of transferring [@hyde1694, 226]. Stewart Culin, two
centuries later, translates it the same way, "the game of transferring"
[@culin1896, 599]. The word is a general one: R. C. Bell notes that
mancala "is used as a generic noun for all the games of the Mankala'h
type" [@bell1969, 115].

**How old it is.** Far less old than is often said. Two claims of great
age have not held up:

- A slab with two rows of six hollows from 'Ain Ghazal in Jordan, from
  the Neolithic, was published in 1992 as a "mancala-type" board.
  Thierry Depaulis points out that the hollows are no more than 3 cm
  across and 2 cm deep, too small for the seeds of any mancala game,
  that nothing was found with it to play with, and that it would leave
  a gap of more than 6,000 years before the first documented boards
  [@depaulis2020, 129-130].
- Holes cut in the roof slabs of the temple at Kurna, on the great
  pylon at Karnak and at Luxor have been given as mancala boards of
  ancient Egypt; Bell repeats them, with the Kurna temple of about
  1400 BC [@bell1969, 114-115]. The report goes back to Henry Parker,
  who was himself unsure "whether the holes at Kurna and Gizeh are of
  very early date or not" [@parker1909, 589-592]. The date is the
  building's, not the holes'. Alex de Voogt notes that the date of a
  monument and the people who built it "are usually not associated with
  the graffiti game board", and that mancala is absent from the written
  and pictorial record of ancient Egypt [@devoogt2021, 6]. Already in
  1694 Hyde told of the traveller Wansleb, who heard from Copts of a
  mancala board on top of the Great Pyramid and went to look, but could
  not find any such thing [@hyde1694, 232].

Boards of three rows of holes, such as the limestone block Flinders
Petrie found at Memphis, which Bell takes for "an early form of
Mankala'h" [@bell1969, 115], are probably not mancala at all. Depaulis
argues that three-row boards "cannot reasonably be mancalas": the one
three-row mancala known, in Ethiopia, is really a two-row board folded
in. They are more likely the race game *tāb* [@depaulis2024, 39-41].
Boards of holes cut into Roman and later buildings are often later
still: de Voogt dates the boards at Palmyra to soldiers garrisoned in
the temple long after Roman times [@devoogt2010], and those on the
pyramids of Meroe in Sudan most likely to soldiers of the nineteenth
century [@devoogt2012].

**The earliest likely boards.** Fragments of an earthenware board and
two-row boards cut in limestone were dug up by Francis Anfray at Matara
in Eritrea, a town of the kingdom of Aksum, and dated to the 6th to 8th
centuries AD; others were found at Yeha and Asmara. Depaulis finds no
earlier such finds anywhere in Africa and calls an Ethiopian cradle for
mancala "a very likely hypothesis"; two-row boards from Byzantine sites
in Israel, of 400 to 700 AD if they are dated right, may be as old or a
little older [@depaulis2024, 40]. De Voogt is more cautious: the Matara
boards are taken for mancala "based solely on appearance", and their
identification "remains speculative". His conclusion is that mancala's
antiquity "goes back hundreds of years but not yet thousands", and that
the games are "of distinct kinds with separate histories"
[@devoogt2021, 1, 3-4].

In 2024 Veronica Waweru of Yale reported about twenty boards of holes cut
in a rock ledge at Lewa in central Kenya [@yale-waweru]. They have not
been dated: they could be several thousand years old, or "just a few
centuries" [@brunskill2024].

**The first words about it.** An Arabic legal text of about 800 AD,
ash-Shāfi'ī's *Kitāb al-Umm*, mentions *hizzah*, "a piece of wood in
which there are holes for playing", which fits a mancala board well
[@depaulis2024, 56]. Culin says the commentary to the tenth-century
*Kitāb al-Aghānī* speaks of "a game like Mancala" [@culin1896, 599]; he
gives no page, and we have not found the passage. Culin also took the
*mancal* that the Portuguese priest Francisco Alvares met in Ethiopia
in the 1520s for the game. In Alvares' text it is a distance: two
houses at Aksum stand "a distance of a game of manqual" apart, and his
translator notes that the Portuguese *mancal* was an old game whose
Spanish rendering is a game of horseshoes [@alvares1881, 84]. It was a
throwing game, not a sowing game.

In 1623 Richard Jobson, trading on the Gambia river, wrote of the men
there that they had "only one kind of game to recreate themselves
withall, and that is in a peece of wood, certaine great holes cut, which
they set upon the ground betwixt two of them, and with a number of some
thirtie pibble stones, after a manner of counting, they take one from
the other, until one is possessed of all" [@jobson1623, 38]. He names no
game and no rows, but no other game fits. Hyde, in 1694, describes two
Mesopotamian forms, a simple one and an intricate one, a box board of two
rows of six holes with thirty-six shells, seven holes a side among the
Arabs, and a board of four rows of eight that he learnt of from the
envoy of the King of Anjouan, in the Comoros [@hyde1694, 226-232]. The
first full rules come from E. W. Lane, who saw the game played for
coffee in the coffee shops of Cairo when he lived in Egypt in the
1830s [@lane1836, 315; @bell1969, 113]; his account is the source of two rule sets here.

**Across the oceans.** Rules travel with the people who play them, and
games played the same way in two places are the best evidence that the
game went from one to the other. De Voogt gives three such links: West
Africa and the Caribbean; the Swahili coast; and the Maldives, whose
game had arrived "before 1153 CE". The earliest written description of
a mancala's rules is Flacourt's, from Madagascar, in 1658
[@devoogt2021, 2, 5]. Enslaved Africans brought the game to the
Americas: Melville Herskovits found it among the Saramaka of Suriname
and in the Caribbean, where it was played in the house of mourning to
amuse the dead, and never after dark, when the spirits of the dead
would join in [@herskovits1932; as cited in @bell1969, 121-122]. In North
America the evidence is thin; smooth stones found with gaming pieces at
the Hermitage plantation in Tennessee may be mancala counters
[@christiano2010, 10-11].

**Modern times.** In 1891 Milton Bradley sold a mancala board in the
United States under the name "Chuba" [@culin1896, 605-607]. Kalah, the
form most Americans know, was W. J. Champion's: he filed for a patent on
its board in 1951 and sold it from the 1950s [@champion1955;
@avedon-kalah]. In 2020 UNESCO inscribed Togyzqumalaq, Toguz Korgool
and Mangala, the mancala games of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Türkiye, on
its list of the intangible heritage of humanity [@unesco-toguz]. Two of
the games here have been solved by computer: Awari (Oware) is a draw
with perfect play [@romein2002], and Kalah with four seeds a pit is won
by the first player [@irving2000]. The program's hardest opponent
searches far but does not play perfectly.

## The family

![The board of the two-rank games: South's pits A to F along the near
side, North's a to f along the far side, the stores at the ends, and
the sowing anticlockwise.](figures/mancala-board.svg){#fig-mancala-board width=85%}

The games in this chapter are all played on two rows of pits, one row
for each player; the four-row games of East and Southern Africa are a
family of their own, for a later version. This book names the pits as
Lane and Bell do: A to F (or further) for the near player's pits, in the
order of sowing, and a to f for the far player's, so that F is followed
by a and f by A [@bell1969, 116-117]. Here the near player is South and
the far player North. Pits facing each other are A and f, B and e, and
so on.

The words used in every section:

- **Sowing.** Lifting all the seeds of a pit and dropping them one at a
  time into the pits that follow, in the direction of play.
- **Lap.** One sowing, from lifting to the last seed. In some games a
  move is one lap; in others the last seed lifts the pit it falls in and
  sows on (a **relay**), or lifts the pit after it (the **Indian lap**).
- **Store.** A pit at the end, where captured seeds are kept. In some
  games the store is sown into as the seeds go past.
- **Capture.** Taking seeds into the store. The games differ most in
  this: by the count the last seed makes (two or three in Oware, an even
  number in Toguz Kumalak), by an empty pit of your own (Kalah), by an
  empty pit after the last seed (Pallanguli).
- **Feeding.** In the Oware family a player must leave the other
  something to play.
- **Grand slam.** A capture of every seed on the other side; the Oware
  family forbids or voids it.

## Oware, Abapa

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 6, 4 seeds in each pit (48), stores not sown |
| Sowing         | One lap, anticlockwise; the pit sown from is passed over |
| Capture        | Last seed makes 2 or 3 on the other side, and back along it |
| Goal           | 25 seeds                                              |

Oware is the game of the Akan of Ghana, and under many names (*awale*,
*ayo*, *wari*, *warri*) the most widely played mancala of West Africa
and the Caribbean. The Oware Society calls Abapa "a traditional variant
of Oware played by adults and used for competitions", its name meaning
"the proper version" in Twi [@owaresoc-abapa]. The program follows the
Society's rules, with Aualé's wording where the Society's is unclear
[@auale-rules].

**Sowing.** South plays from any pit of the near row that holds seeds,
North from the far row. All the seeds of the pit are sown one by one
into the following pits, anticlockwise. A pit with more than eleven
seeds goes round the whole board: the pit it came from is passed over,
and the sowing goes on in the next one.

**Capture.** If the last seed makes a pit on the other side hold exactly
two or three, those seeds are captured; so are the seeds of the pit
before it if it too now holds two or three, and so on back along the
other side until a pit holds any other number, or the sowing reaches the
mover's own row.

```{.example file="mancala/oware-capture"}
```

**The grand slam.** A capture may not take every seed on the other side:
the Society allows "a maximum of five houses"; "beyond five houses one
forfeits everything, as this would leave the opponent with no seeds to
play with". Such a move is sown, but it captures nothing.

```{.example file="mancala/oware-grand-slam"}
```

*Our choice:* the program follows the reason the Society gives. A capture
that would leave the other side with no seed at all takes nothing, even
when it takes only five houses because the sixth was already empty;
Aualé says the same [@auale-rules].

**Feeding.** A player whose opponent has no seeds must make a move that
gives them some; "a move that does not do this whilst being able to feed
the other player with seeds is not allowed". *Our choice,* where the
Society is silent: if no move can feed the other side, the game ends and
the player takes the seeds on their own side, as Aualé has it.

**The end.** The first to capture 25 seeds wins; 24 each is a draw. When
the seeds only go round with no capture possible, "each player keeps the
seeds on their side". *Our choice:* the program takes the same position
coming round for the third time to be that point.

## Wari, after Bell

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 6, 4 seeds in each pit (48)                 |
| Sowing         | One lap, anticlockwise; the cup sown from is passed over |
| Capture        | Last seed makes 2 or 3 on the other side, and back along it |
| Goal           | The most seeds                                        |

R. C. Bell gives Wari from Ghana, with a board drawn from R. S.
Rattray's *Religion and Art in Ashanti*, and his own six rules, with
worked examples, tactics and a notation for recording games
[@bell1969, 116-121]. Rattray's book has a chapter on the game by G. T.
Bennett, which we have not been able to read [@bennett1927; as cited in
@ludii-oware].

The rules are Oware's with three differences, all from Bell:

- **No capture may take everything.** "A player is not, however, allowed
  to capture all the pieces on the opponent's side of the board as this
  would leave him nothing to lift at the next turn of play". The Ludii
  summary of Bennett's chapter also says such a move "is not allowed"
  [@ludii-oware]. So the program does not let it be played. *Our
  choice,* where Bell is silent: if every move a player has would take
  everything, any of them may be played and it captures, as Romein and
  Bal play Awari [@romein2002, 165].
- **Feeding.** "When an opponent's cups are empty a player must, if
  possible, feed seeds into them. If he cannot do so the game is over
  and all the seeds left on the board become the player's."
- **The end.** The game ends when a player whose turn it is has no seeds,
  and "when only a few seeds remain in play they may circulate with no
  captures possible for either player. Each then takes the seeds on his
  own side of the board." The most seeds wins. (*Our choice,* as for
  Oware: the third time a position comes round.)

Bell adds "match play": the winner of a game fills the holes of his own
side, then begins to fill the other's, and every hole he fills becomes
part of his side for the next game, until the loser concedes. *Our
choice:* the program plays single games.

## Mankal'ah, the game of the ignorant

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 6, 72 pebbles set out by hand               |
| Sowing         | Always from the last loaded pit; 3, 5 or more sow on    |
| Capture        | Last pebble makes 2 or 4, on either side, with the pits facing |
| Goal           | 60 points over several boards                          |

E. W. Lane describes mankal'ah as "one of the games most common among
the Egyptians", played on a board of twelve holes with seventy-two
cowries or pebbles. There are "two modes of playing it": the simple one,
"called 'the game of the ignorant'", and "the game of the wise, or
intelligent" [@lane1836, 315]. The program offers both, as Lane gives
them.

**Setting out.** One player distributes all the pebbles "unequally into
the beyts; generally putting at least four into each", without counting.
"If they were distributed equally, there would be six in each beyt; but
this is seldom done: for, in this case, he who plays first is sure to
lose." The other, if he does not like the distribution, "may turn the
board round; and then his adversary begins the game". *Our choices:* who
sets out the first board is decided by lot; the computer sets out four in
each pit and the rest at random; a person may set them out by hand or at
random.

**Sowing.** The player begins "from beyt F, or, if that be empty, from
the nearest beyt to it in his own row containing one or more haswehs",
and sows round the board. Where the last pebble falls decides what
follows:

- it makes **one** (the pit was empty): the turn ends;
- it makes **two or four**: the player "takes its contents, with those of
  the beyt opposite", and so with each pit before it holding two or four,
  "no beyt with any other number intervening", each with the pit facing
  it; the turn ends;
- it makes **three, or five, or more**: the player "takes these out, and
  goes on distributing them in the same manner as before".

*Our choice:* Lane does not limit the captures to one side, so the
program takes twos and fours on either side.

**When a side runs dry.** "When one party has more than a single hasweh
in one or more of his beyts, and the other has none, the former is
obliged to put one of his into the first of his adversary's beyts. If
only one hasweh remain on one side, and none on the other, that one is
the property of the person on whose side it is." *Our choice:* the pebble
given comes from the giver's loaded pit nearest the crossing.

**Scoring.** When the board is cleared, the one who has taken more
"reckons the excess of his above his adversary's number as his gain".
"The gainer in one board begins to play the next board; his adversary
having first distributed the hasa. When either party has made his
successive gains amount to sixty, he has won the game." Lane adds that
the loser paid for the coffee.

Bell retells Lane's account, but runs the two modes together and adds a
rule Lane does not give: that after the first capture a player could play
from any pit, and after any other capture had to lift the next loaded pit
[@bell1969, 113-114]. Lane's own text has no such rule; the program
follows Lane.

## Mankal'ah, the game of the wise

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 6, 72 pebbles in matching pits on both sides |
| Sowing         | From any pit; 3, 5 or more sow on                       |
| Capture        | Last pebble makes 2 or 4, on either side, with the pits facing |
| Goal           | 60 points over several boards                          |

The game "the wise, or intelligent" play differs in two things
[@lane1836, 315]:

- **Setting out.** "The hasa are distributed in one or more beyts on one
  side, and in the corresponding beyt or beyts on the other side;
  commonly in four beyts on each side, leaving the two extreme beyts of
  each side vacant: or ... about half into beyt A, and the remainder in
  beyt a." The distributor does not count them, and the other may still
  turn the board round.
- **Choosing.** "The person who plays first may begin from any one of his
  beyts; judging by his eye which will bring him the best fortune." After
  the first gain a player may count the pebbles in his pits "and plays
  from that which will bring him the greatest advantage".

Everything else is as in the game of the ignorant. *Our choices:* the
computer sets out one of Lane's two common arrangements, at random; a
person setting out by hand puts each pebble in a pit and its partner on
the other side at once. Counting is always allowed: the program shows
every number.

## Kalah

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 6, 4 seeds in each pit, stores sown         |
| Sowing         | One lap, anticlockwise, through your own store         |
| Capture        | Last seed in an empty pit of your own takes the pit facing it |
| Goal           | 40 points over several games                           |

Kalah is not an old game but a modern American one, and the one most
people now call Mancala. W. J. Champion of Mystic, Connecticut, filed for
a patent on "a new and improved game board for playing the game of Kalah
or Mop-up" in 1951; it was granted in 1955 [@champion1955], and the
Kalah Game Company sold the game from the 1950s [@avedon-kalah]. The
program follows the patent's rules.

**Sowing.** The seeds of a pit are sown anticlockwise, "toward his bowl
or kalah", into the player's own store as they pass it and on into the
other side; only the opponent's store is passed over.

**Another move.** If the last seed falls in the player's own store, the
player moves again.

```{.example file="mancala/kalah-store"}
```

**Capture.** If the last seed falls in an empty pit on the player's own
side, the seeds in the pit facing it are captured, and the capturing
seed with them, into the player's store. *Our choice:* the patent does
not say what happens when the pit facing is empty; the program then
captures nothing, which is how Kalah is most often played.

```{.example file="mancala/kalah-capture"}
```

**The end and the score.** A game ends when all the pits on one side are
empty; the seeds left on the other side go to that side's store. The
patent scores a game by the seeds won from the opponent: put back the
original number in each pit and count what is left over. The first to 40
points wins the series, and the first move alternates from game to game.
The patent allows three seeds a pit "for a short game or young
players", and four to six for adults; the program plays four.

## Congkak, after Hellier

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 7, 7 seeds in each hole (98), stores sown    |
| Sowing         | Clockwise; the last seed in a loaded hole or your own store goes on |
| Capture        | Last seed in an empty hole of your own takes the hole facing it |
| Goal           | Leave the other player unable to fill a single hole    |

Congkak is the mancala of the Malay world, played on a board "shaped
like a junk or boat"; it is *congklak* and *dakon* in Indonesia and
*sungka* in the Philippines. In 1907 M. Hellier described the game as it
was played at the courts of the Malay rajas, where it was a women's game,
in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society
[@hellier1907, 93-94]. Hellier's is the earliest full account of the
Malay rules we have read, and the program follows it.

**The board.** "The board has 7 holes on each side, with a larger hole or
compartment at each end." Each player has one "village", her row, and
places seven seeds in each hole. Play goes from right to left along a
player's own row, clockwise round the board, and a player's store, her
"house", is the large hole on her left.

**Sowing.** "Each player then takes all the seeds from any one of the
other holes in her 'village' and moving as before from right to left
around the board again drops a seed into each hole, taking care to drop
one into her own house but none into her opponent's." Where the last
seed falls decides what follows:

- in a hole with **other seeds**, on either side: "these are taken and
  the player continues in play", sowing them on;
- in **her own house**: "she also continues in play, taking the seeds
  from any hole in her 'village'";
- in an **empty hole**: "the player is dead (mati)". If the hole is in
  her own village, "any seeds in the opposite hole on her opponent's side
  may be taken and put into the 'house'".


**Ruined wells.** "When no more seeds remain in a player's 'village' she
is said to be 'once defeated'". She takes the seeds in her house and
fills her holes again, seven to a hole; "should any holes be left empty
they are called 'ruined wells'". The other player plays on as before.
"The game goes on in this way until a player has lost all her seeds. She
is then 'utterly destroyed'."

**Our choices.**

- Hellier's players play at the same time: "The players start together",
  and a player who is dead "must wait until the other player is dead
  before she can again play". The program takes turns: each turn lasts
  until the player is dead, and South begins. The opening, in which both
  players sow the hole on their right into their own house, becomes
  South's first move.
- Hellier does not say which holes become ruined wells. The program fills
  the holes in the order of sowing, from the right, and closes the rest;
  no seed is sown into a ruined well, as in the Ceylonese game Overbeck
  describes, where unfilled holes "remain empty and are not used"
  [@overbeck1915, 9].
- Hellier does not say whether the capturing seed goes into the house
  with the seeds taken. The program leaves it in its hole.
- A player who cannot fill a single hole has lost all her seeds: the
  game ends, and the other player takes what is left on the board.

**Other accounts.** The game was not the same everywhere. Skeat gives a
board of eight holes a side [@skeat1900, 486]. Culin's Philippine
*chungcajon* has seven holes of seven shells, but no captures, and the
most shells in the home wins [@culin1900, 653-654]. Overbeck's notes of
1915 add the capture of the last seed with the opposite hole and the
rule that the last to move starts the next game, but the rules he gives
are those of Ceylon, with nine holes; for the Malay game he defers to
Hellier [@overbeck1915, 7-10]. Henry Parker's Colombo game of the same
kind is played in one round, and a last shell in the store ends the turn
[@parker1909, 599-600].

## Pallanguli, after Durai

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 7, 6 seeds in each hole (84)                 |
| Sowing         | Anticlockwise; after the last seed the next hole is lifted and sown |
| Capture        | An empty hole after the last seed: the hole beyond it; a hole of four, to its owner |
| Goal           | Leave the other player unable to fill a single hole    |

Pallanguli is the Tamil game of South India and Sri Lanka. In 1928 Mrs
H. G. Durai described it in *Man*, the journal of the Royal
Anthropological Institute [@durai1928, 185-186]. R. C. Bell's account
follows hers closely [@bell1969, 115-116]. The program follows Durai.

**Sowing.** The players place "six pieces in each of the seven holes on
their own side". A player lifts the pieces of any hole of her own and,
"moving always in a counter-clockwise direction, places one piece in
each of the holes as he goes". Then, unlike every other game in this
chapter, the next hole decides: "Having deposited the last piece in a
hole containing others (either on his own or his opponent's side) he
picks up all the pieces in the hole next the one he ended in and
proceeds as before".

**Capture.** "The move ends when he puts his last piece into a hole next
to an empty one. When he does this he captures all the pieces in the
hole on the further side of the empty one ... If this hole is also empty
the move ends, but he captures nothing."

**Cows.** "As soon as the number of pieces in a hole becomes 'four' the
players on whose side the 'four' is removes them from the board and adds
them to the pieces already captured. A 'four' is called a *pasu*, which
means a 'cow'." A cow goes to the owner of the hole, whoever sowed it.

**Rounds.** Play goes on until the board is cleared. Then each player
"puts back his winnings, six to a hole, into as many holes on his own
side as he can fill". The holes the loser cannot fill are marked with
"a bit of paper or leaf or other rubbish to show that it is not in use":
they are *peekkuli*, dung holes, and are left out of the next round.
Pieces left over stay in the store. "The right to play first alternates
round by round", and a player who wins enough may open her rubbish holes
again. "The game goes on until eventually one or other of the players has
not even enough pieces to fill one hole."

**Where Bell differs.** Bell has play go on after a capture, "from the
next loaded hole beyond" [@bell1969, 115]. Durai, his source, ends the
move there; the program follows Durai.

**Our choices.**

- Durai speaks of a last piece in a hole "containing others". If the last
  piece falls in an empty hole and the next hole is loaded, the program
  lifts the next hole too, as Bell has it ("when the last seed of a lift
  falls into a hole, either on his own side of the board or his
  opponent's, he picks up all the pieces in the next hole").
- Durai does not say which holes become rubbish holes. The program fills
  a player's holes in the order of sowing and closes the rest.
- If a player has no pieces on her side when it is her turn, the round
  ends and the pieces still on the board go to the other player.
- A move could in principle go round for ever; the program ends a move
  after 500 laps.

Modern descriptions of *pallanguzhi* differ again: twelve seeds a hole
with two in the middle ones, or five, or seven, and a cow of six to the
sower. We have not found a source for them as good as Durai, and the
program does not offer them.

## Toguz Kumalak

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 9, 9 stones in each hole (162), two kazans   |
| Sowing         | One lap, anticlockwise; the first stone goes back into its hole |
| Capture        | Last stone makes a hole on the other side even; three makes a tuzdyk |
| Goal           | 82 stones                                             |

Toguz Kumalak, "nine stones" (in Kyrgyz Toguz Korgool), is the game of
the Kazakh and Kyrgyz steppe, played in national federations and world
championships and inscribed on UNESCO's list of intangible heritage in
2020 with the Turkish Mangala [@unesco-toguz]. The program follows the
competition rules approved in Kazakhstan in 2013 [@togyz2013]; the
quotations are our translation from the Kazakh.

**Sowing.** A player takes all the stones of one of her holes, "leaves
one" in it, and sows the rest anticlockwise from the next hole on. A
hole with a single stone may be played: the stone moves to the next
hole, and its own hole is left empty.

**Capture.** If the last stone makes a hole on the other side even (2, 4,
6 and so on), its stones are captured into the player's kazan, her store.
A last stone on her own side, or making an odd number, captures nothing.

**Tuzdyk.** If the last stone falls in a hole of the opponent's holding
two, making three, "except No. 9", those three are captured and the hole
becomes the player's *tuzdyk*. From then on every stone that falls into
it goes, "obligatorily", into her kazan. A player may make a tuzdyk "only
once" in a game, never in the opponent's ninth hole, and never in the hole
whose number matches the opponent's tuzdyk. A tuzdyk is never played
from.

**The end.** The game ends as soon as a player has 82 stones; 81 each is
a draw. A player who has no move is in *atsyrau*: "the opponent of the
player in atsyrau makes an extra move and puts all the stones into his
kazan", and the stones are counted. *Our choice:* the program gives the
opponent the stones at once, without the extra move; the count comes out
the same, unless the extra move would have given the other a move again,
in which case the federation's later text says play goes on
[@togyz-federation].

Older accounts differ in two things: the English Wikipedia lets a player
move the stones of her tuzdyk to her kazan when she likes, and gives the
stones left at the end to the side they are on. The Kazakh rules do
neither.

## Bohnenspiel

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Board          | 2 rows of 6, 6 beans in each pit (72)                  |
| Sowing         | One lap, anticlockwise                                 |
| Capture        | Last bean makes 2, 4 or 6, on either side, and back along the row |
| Goal           | The most beans                                        |

Fritz Jahn tells in *Alte deutsche Spiele*, *Old German Games*, of 1917
how he rediscovered the bean game, "this age-old, most original and
interesting board game, entirely forgotten", in 1908, visiting Baron von
Stackelberg at Kardis in the Baltic provinces. More than a hundred years
before, the Shah of Persia had given a bean game to the Empress
Catherine II of Russia, and a copy of it had come to Kardis; Jahn had his
own made exactly after it [@jahn1917, 14]. He describes "two times six
bean cups facing each other", a winning hollow at each player's right
hand, and two times thirty-six beans, six in each cup. The aim, in our
translation: when counting out the beans "to the right, over one's own
cups and the opponent's", to make "as many twos or fours or sixes as
possible lie in one's own or the opponent's cups without a break", which
are then "brought home as winnings into one's winning hollow"
[@jahn1917, 15].

So captures are of two, four or six, on **either side**, in an unbroken
run. Jahn leaves the rest to the players. *Our choices,* with the rules
igGameCenter gives [@igg-bohnenspiel]:

- the winning hollows are not sown into;
- the run is counted back from the pit of the last bean, through the
  pits before it, while each holds two, four or six;
- a player who cannot move ends the game, and the beans left on the board
  go to the other player;
- Jahn does not say whether a sowing of more than eleven beans passes
  over the pit it came from; the program sows into it.

## Playing it in the terminal

The program draws the board with South along the bottom and North along
the top, as in the figures of this chapter; in Congkak, which goes
clockwise, it is drawn the other way round. Each pit shows its count
and, while they fit, its seeds. The stores are at the ends, and a hand
carries the seeds round when a move is made, seed by seed. Any key or a
click finishes the move at once.

- **Left and Right** move the cursor along your row; **Enter** sows the
  pit under it. Or click a pit.
- In Lane's games the pebbles are set out first. The player setting
  out moves the cursor with Left and Right (and Tab to the other row in
  the game of the ignorant), puts a pebble in with Up and takes one out
  with Down, or presses **R** to set out the rest at random; **Enter**
  when the hand is empty. The other player then presses **T** to turn
  the board round, or Enter to keep it.
- **M** returns to the menu, **F1** shows these rules, **Q** quits.

The computer plays at three strengths. Easy looks one move ahead and
sometimes plays any move at all; normal searches four moves deep; hard
searches as deep as it can in a second and a half. A move that gives the
player another move counts as part of the same turn in its search.
