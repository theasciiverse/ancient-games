# Patolli

|                |                                                       |
|:---------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Played         | The Aztecs of central Mexico, before and just after the Spanish conquest (1521) |
| Players        | Two (Durán: "the usual way"); more in partnership     |
| Kind           | Race and betting game: six stones once round a painted cross |
| Program        | `patolli`                                             |
| Rule sets      | Bell's suggested rules (1969), on Durán's equipment   |

::: brief
A cross painted on a mat, its arms divided into compartments; six
stones each, red and blue; five black beans, each with one white hole.
Cast the beans: the holes showing count 1 to 4, all five 10. Each
player brings his stones in on his own square of the middle and moves
them once round the cross, the way his first stone took, and off
beside where they came in. A compartment holds one stone; nobody is
taken. The pinched compartments cost two forfeits, the rounded ends give
another throw. The first with all six stones off wins the pool. The
rules are a reconstruction, R. C. Bell's; the chroniclers give only the
equipment and the count. The rules in short follow; the full rules, the
sources and the history are in RULES.txt and the rulebook.
:::

## What survives

No writer of the 16th century gives the rules of patolli. What the
chroniclers give is the equipment, the count of the throw, and what the
game meant to those who played it. R. C. Bell put it plainly: "Little
more is known about patolli and this is not enough to play the game"
[@bell1969, 7].

**Diego Durán**, a Dominican friar in Mexico, wrote the fullest
account, about 1579, in his book of the old rites
[@duran1880, 234-238; our translations]. On a mat "they had painted a
great saltire that took the mat from corner to corner; across the
inside of the saltire were drawn lines that served as houses", ruled in
melted rubber. "For these houses there were twelve small stones, six red
and six blue ... if two played, which was the usual way, one took six
and the other the other six; and though many played, one always played
for all." The lots were "some black beans, five or ten as they wished
to lose or win, which had little white holes, one in each bean ... if
one, one; if two, two; if three, three; if four, four; but five showing
were ten, and ten, twenty"; "and so those little white spots were the
lots, and the count of the lines won, and for moving the stones from
one house to another" [@duran1880, 235].

**The god of the beans.** "The name of the god of the dice was
Macuilxochitl, which means five roses"; there were five beans in his
honour, and as they cast them on the mat the players "called aloud
'Macuilxochitl!' and gave a great clap of the hands, and then looked to
see what points had come" [@duran1880, 237]. Gamesters carried the mat
rolled under the arm and the beans tied in a cloth, spoke to them "a
thousand words of love", and offered incense and food before they
played [@duran1880, 235-236]. "So many onlookers and gamesters came
that they were on top of one another over the mat, some to play, others
to bet" [@duran1880, 235].

8, about 1577, vol. 2 fol. 269r. Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana,
Florence, Med. Palat. 219. Public domain.](images/patolli-florentine-codex.jpg){width=45%}

**Playing oneself into slavery.** Durán's chapter is "of the games
these Indians had to amuse and divert themselves on feast days, but
also to stake themselves and become slaves for ever"
[@duran1880, 234]: when nothing else was left, "they staked themselves
at a price, on condition that if within a certain time they could not
redeem themselves, they would remain the slave for ever of the one who
won them" [@duran1880, 237]. Gómara, the first to describe the game in
print (1552), and Torquemada say the same [@gomara1870, 224;
@torquemada1723, 554].

**Bernardino de Sahagún** counts patolli among the lords' pastimes: "a
game called patolli, which is like the game of castro or alquerque, or
almost like dice; there are four large beans, each with a hole, and
they throw them by hand on a mat ... where a figure is made"; elsewhere
he has three beans [@sahagun1829, 292, 317; our translation]. The game
and the ball game "they have given up, as suspect of some idolatrous
superstitions" [@sahagun1829, 292]; the magistrates tore the mats and
"burned on their hands the little beans that served as dice", so that,
Durán writes, no memory of the game was left [@duran1880, 238].

**Moctezuma** is often said to have played patolli. Gómara says only
that "sometimes Moctezuma watched them play" [@gomara1870, 223]; the
game Bernal Díaz saw him play with Cortés was *totoloque*, pellets of
gold thrown at a mark [@diazdelcastillo1632, cap. XCVII].

**What is not known.** How the stones went round, what the marked
compartments did, how a game was won: nothing in the chronicles says.
The pictures show an X with arms two compartments wide, hourglass marks
near the ends and rounded ends, but they differ in the number of
compartments. The 52 compartments of many modern accounts, said to
match the 52 years of the Aztec calendar round, come from 20th-century
reconstructions, Mateos Higuera's of 1930 and Swezey's of 1970, not
from any chronicle [@swezey1983, 375; @mateoshiguera1930 as cited in
@swezey1983]; so does the rule that a bean standing on its edge wins
all [@kendall1980]. The many square boards scratched in the floors of
Teotihuacan and the Maya cities, often called patolli boards, are of
another game: "none offers the cross-shaped board typical of patolli"
[@depaulis2018, 42]. Edward Tylor argued in 1879 that patolli came from
the Indian pachisi [@tylor1879, 124-125]; the question has stayed open.

## The board

![The cross drawn upright with its 60 compartments numbered round the
ring from Red's square of the middle; the pinched compartments
marked with their triangles, the rounded ends ringed.](figures/patolli-board.svg){width=60%}

Bell's figure, "after Duran" [@bell1969, 6], is the board here: four
arms, each two rows of seven compartments, the last of each row half of
the arm's rounded end, round four squares in the middle; in every row
the fifth and sixth compartments from the middle are pinched by black
triangles, an hourglass across the arm. Bell's text gives "sixteen
compartments" a limb [@bell1969, 7]; his drawing agrees only if the two
squares of the middle beside each limb are counted with it. Round the
outline of the cross the compartments make a ring of 60. The program
draws the cross upright; on the mat it lay corner to corner.

## Bell's suggested rules

::: brief
- Each casts the beans; the higher begins (ties cast again).
- Your first throw brings a stone in on your square of the middle and
  moves it the whole throw, either way round; all your stones then go
  that way. After that a stone comes in only on a throw of 1.
- A throw moves one stone its whole count. No stone may land on a
  compartment that holds a stone (it may pass over). If any stone can
  move, one must.
- No move: a forfeit into the pool. Landing on a pinched compartment:
  two forfeits to the other player. Landing on a rounded end: another
  throw.
- Stones go once round and off, beside where they came in, by the exact
  throw; the other pays a forfeit for each. The first with all six off
  wins the pool.
- Our choices: a pool of 10 each and purses of 20; a throw of no holes
  moves nothing and costs nothing; a player who cannot pay in full pays
  what he has.
:::

Bell's "Suggested Additional Rules (for two players)" [@bell1969, 7-8],
in his words:

1. "The players put an agreed sum into a pool and decide on the size of
   the forfeits."
2. "One player takes six red pieces, the other six blue."
3. "Each player casts the patolli in turn and the higher scorer starts
   the game by casting again."
4. "The opening player introduces a piece on to his nearest central
   square and then moves it the indicated number of compartments in
   either direction round the board, but whichever way his first piece
   moves the others must do likewise. His opponent at his opening throw
   also has a free choice of direction but having once chosen, the
   remaining pieces must travel the same way."
5. "After the entry of the first piece the others must enter the board
   on the player's nearest central square with a throw of 1."
6. "No piece may move on to a compartment occupied by any other piece."
7. "If two or more of a player's pieces can be moved to satisfy a
   particular throw, the player has free choice which it shall be, but
   if only one piece can move, the move must be made even if it is to
   the player's disadvantage."
8. "If a player cannot move any piece he pays a forfeit into the pool."
9. "A player landing on a compartment which has been reduced in size by
   the markings pays two forfeits to his opponent."
10. "A player moving a piece on to one of the rounded compartments at
    the end of the limbs of the cross is awarded another turn."
11. "Pieces travel round the cross and are borne off on the side of the
    fourth limb nearest the player with exact throws, as in
    backgammon. The four central squares, which have been traversed in
    the circuit of the piece, are excluded in the bearing off."
12. "As each of the player's pieces leaves the cross his opponent pays
    him one forfeit."
13. "The player removing all his pieces from the cross first wins the
    stake in the pool."

Rules 8 to 10 rest on a guess, which Bell gives: "By comparison with
other North American games it is probable that the marked squares were
penalty areas and the player was penalized for trespassing on
them" [@bell1969, 7]. His rules 14 and 15, for three and four players,
are not offered.

**Our choices**, where Bell is silent:

- **The stakes**: each puts 10 into the pool and has 20 in his purse; a
  forfeit is 1. A player who cannot pay in full pays what he has, and
  the game goes on: only bearing off ends it.
- **No holes up** moves nothing and costs nothing; it is not a throw
  that "cannot move" (Durán gives no value for it).
- **Passing**: a stone may pass over others; Bell forbids only landing.
- **Another turn** on a rounded end is one more cast.
- **The way round**: Red enters on the lower left square of the middle
  and Blue on the upper right, opposite; each chooses his way with his
  first stone. A stone goes off from the last compartment before its own
  square of the middle, which it does not enter again.

**Not offered.** A game on the 52-compartment route of Swezey's
reconstruction, three arms of the cross for each player
[@swezey1983, 375, fig. 17b]: almost everything in it but the route
would be our own invention. Kendall's and Mateos Higuera's rules, the
likely source of today's popular ones, could not be read
[@kendall1980; @mateoshiguera1930].

## The ornament

Durán and Sahagún both say the board was painted on a mat. In the patolli
scene of the Codex Magliabechiano (fol. 60r), the players sit on mats
woven in a herringbone of short strokes, and five gold discs are drawn
above the board [@nuttall1903]. The codex does not say what the discs
mean, and the program does not guess. Its mat carries that weave in the
four quarters beside the cross, and on a terminal larger than 100 by 30
the five discs stand in a row above it, as in the codex. Both are drawn in braille dots, in
dim tones, never over a stone, a highlight or a number. They come from
the sixteenth-century pictures, not from the living Nahua tradition; the
step-fret often put on patolli boards today is in none of the patolli
pictures and is not used. O turns the ornament off and on, and
`patolli --plain` starts without it.

## Playing it in the terminal

::: brief
- Enter or Space: cast the beans. Then arrows (or the mouse) to a lit
  compartment, Enter to send a stone there; B (or the "bear off"
  button) to bear a stone off.
- The panel shows the beans as they fell (◉ a hole up), the purses and
  the pool, and which way each side goes.
- When the game is over, Enter plays again.
- M: menu; F1: this help; Q: quit.
- O: the ornament off and on (the mats and discs of the Codex
  Magliabechiano's patolli scene); `--plain` starts without it.
:::

Start `patolli`, choose whom to play against (the computer, easy,
normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side you
play. Who begins is cast for.

| Key | |
|:--|:--|
| Enter or Space | Cast the beans; send a stone to the lit compartment under the cursor |
| Arrows | Move the cursor round the cross |
| B | Bear a stone off |
| Mouse | Click to cast; click a lit compartment, or a stone |
| F1 or ? | The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc closes them) |
| Enter or Space, after a game | Play again |
| M, or Esc after a game | Back to the menu |
| O | The ornament off and on |
| Q | Quit |

The computer: easy moves its furthest stone, and now and then any;
normal judges the race and the purses, and likes the rounded ends;
hard also weighs every cast the other side could make next, and its
best answer.

`patolli --demo` lets the computer play both sides; `--seed N` repeats
a game exactly; `--plain` leaves out the ornament.
