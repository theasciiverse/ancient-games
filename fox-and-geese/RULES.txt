What survives
-------------

The game. One player has a single fox, the other a flock of geese. The fox
kills geese by jumping over them; the geese cannot kill, and try to hem the
fox in until he cannot move. R. C. Bell counts it among the war games of the
tafl group, which “originated in northern Europe and are miniature battles
fought between unequal forces” (Bell 1969, 1:75-76). The same idea is played
under many names: Wikipedia lists Renard et les poules in France, Fuchs und
Gänse or Fuchs und Henne in Germany, Rävspel in Sweden and Refskák in
Iceland, among others (Wikipedia 2026b).

Early mentions. Nothing written about the game before modern times gives its
rules. Bell collects what there is (Bell 1969, 1:76-77):

- Hala-tafl, “the Fox Game”, is mentioned in the Icelandic Grettis saga,
  which the literary historian Finnur Jónsson believed was written after
  AD 1300. We have not found the passage ourselves.
- During the reign of Edward IV of England (1461 to 1483) the accounts of
  the Royal Household record the purchase “of two foxes and twenty-six
  hounds of silver over-gilt for two sets of Marelles”. Wikipedia takes
  these to have been sets of Fox and Hounds (Wikipedia 2026b).
- Fiske suggested that Fox and Geese may be the same game as Freystafl,
  mentioned in the late sagas.

Boards and sets. The board is a cross of 33 points. In the eighteenth
century, Bell writes, a new game reached England from the Continent:
Solitaire, a patience for one player “using a traditional Fox and Geese
board” (Bell 1969, 1:111). Printed and boxed sets survive from the
nineteenth century, some of them with their rules (see Other variants).

What is not known. How the game was played in the Middle Ages, or whether
the medieval fox games were this game at all. The rules below are the ones
R. C. Bell gives in Board and Table Games from Many Civilizations, first
published in 1960 and read here in the second edition (1969, 1:76). He gives
two forms, an early one with thirteen geese and a later one with seventeen;
the program offers both.

The board with the pieces as they start: the thirteen geese and the fox of
Bell’s figure 62, and dashed, the four more geese of his figure 63.

Thirty-three points in the shape of a cross: a square of three by three in
the middle with an arm of two rows of three on each side. Lines join each
point to its neighbours along the rows and columns. Bell’s figures have no
diagonal lines, and neither does the program: a piece only ever moves up,
down, left or right.

This book names the points as Bell does when he shows the same board for
Solitaire: columns A to G from left to right, rows 1 to 7 from top to
bottom, so that the centre is D4 (Bell 1969, 1:111). The program draws the
board the same way round, the geese at the bottom, but shows no names.

Bell, thirteen geese
--------------------

Bell’s early form (Bell 1969, 1:76):

1.  Thirteen geese stand on the seven points of row 5 and the six points of
    the lower arm, as in Bell’s figure 62. The fox stands on D3.
2.  The players move in turn, one piece a turn. The geese move first.
3.  The fox and the geese move one point along a line to the next point, in
    any direction, onto an empty point.
4.  Instead of stepping, the fox may jump over a goose next to him along a
    line, onto the empty point straight beyond it. The goose is killed and
    taken off the board.
5.  Two or more geese can be killed in one move by a series of such short
    jumps.
6.  The geese cannot jump over the fox.
7.  If the fox cannot move, he loses: the geese win.
8.  If the fox thins the geese out until they are too few to trap him, he
    wins. In the program that is when three or fewer geese are left.

book is built: the position is set up, the engine lists the moves it allows,
and one is played. F is the fox, G a goose, . an empty point.

Bell adds: “If the geese are correctly played the fox must lose.” In his
text the sentence follows the description of this thirteen-geese game and
comes just before the seventeen-geese form (Bell 1969, 1:76). Wikipedia says
the opposite, that the traditional game with thirteen geese “gives the
advantage to the fox”, without a source for it (Wikipedia 2026b).

Our choices:

- The fox starts on D3. Bell’s text says the fox “is placed on any vacant
  point”; both of his figures show it on D3, and the program always puts it
  there.
- The geese move first. Bell does not say who starts. (The Kronheim set of
  the 1840s lets the fox move first: see Other variants.)
- The fox need not capture: he may step when a jump is possible. Bell does
  not say either way.
- In a series of jumps the fox may change direction between jumps and may
  stop after any of them. The geese he kills stay on the board until the
  move ends, so none can be jumped twice, and the fox may not land on any
  point twice in one move, his starting point included.
- Bell’s “unable to trap him” is counted as three geese or fewer. Every way
  of trapping the fox on this board takes at least four geese: the program’s
  tests try every placement of three geese around the fox on every point and
  find none that holds him. With four or more the game goes on, even if the
  geese can no longer trap him in practice.
- If the geese cannot move on their turn, the fox wins. Bell does not say.
- The same position with the same side to move, a third time, is a draw.
  Bell gives no end to a game that goes round in circles.

Bell, seventeen geese
---------------------

“In later forms of the game the geese were increased to seventeen, but were
deprived of the power of moving backwards” (Bell 1969, 1:76). The rules are
those of the thirteen-geese game, with all its choices, but for these
differences:

1.  Seventeen geese: the thirteen of the early form, and two more down each
    side, on A3, A4, G3 and G4 (Bell’s figure 63). The fox again stands on
    D3.
2.  A goose moves forward or sideways, never backward. The fox still moves
    any way.

Our choice: Bell does not say which way is forward. Here it is up the board,
towards row 1; backward is towards row 7, where the geese start. Sideways
moves stay, since Bell takes away only the backward one. With no move back,
geese can now be left with no move at all; then the fox wins, as above.

Example. The same goose, under both forms:

    The goose on D4, the geese to move.
    Position:
         A B C D E F G
      1      . F .
      2      . . .
      3  . . . . . . .
      4  . . . G . . .
      5  . . . . . . .
      6      . . .
      7      . . .
    Bell, thirteen geese: D4 to C4, D3, D5, E4.
    Bell, seventeen geese: D4 to C4, D3, E4.

Other variants
--------------

None of these is in the program.

Kronheim’s rules, 1840s. The box of J. M. Kronheim & Co.’s Fox & Goose Game
held eighteen geese and one fox, and its rules read (J. M. Kronheim & Co.
1840s): “Place the Fox in the centre. The Geese to occupy one half the
Board, with the exception of one hole on each side of the Fox. The Fox moves
first along any marked line, the Geese follow him with a view of blocking
him in. The Fox can take any Goose next him that has a vacant hole behind;
and when the Geese are reduced to six he cannot be blocked and wins the
game.” So here the fox starts in the centre, moves first, and wins with six
geese left, not three.

Diagonal lines. Some boards add diagonals. The board given away with the
Boy’s Own Paper in 1879 has them through every other point (The Boy’s Own
Paper 1879). Wikipedia describes the English game this way: the fox in the
middle, thirteen geese on one side, both moving to any empty point next to
them, diagonals included; capturing is not compulsory; the fox wins when the
geese left cannot surround him. It also mentions versions with 15, 17 or 18
geese, or with two foxes (Wikipedia 2026b).

Halatafl. As Wikipedia describes the Scandinavian fox game still played with
pegs: two foxes and twenty sheep; the sheep move only forward or sideways
and win by filling a square of nine holes, the paddock; the foxes must
capture, and must go on jumping while they can (Wikipedia 2026b).

Asalto. On the same cross of 33 points, two officers defend a fortress, the
top arm, against 24 rebels. The rebels move only towards the fortress; they
win by trapping or capturing both officers, or by filling the fortress. An
officer who fails to make a capture he could have made is removed (“huffed”)
(Wikipedia 2026a).

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start fox-and-geese, choose a rule set, whom to play against (the computer,
easy, normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side you
play.

  Key                            
  ------------------------------ --------------------------------------------------
  Arrows                         Move the cursor
  Enter or Space                 Pick up a piece, then put it down on a lit point
  Esc                            Let go of the piece
  Mouse                          Click a piece, then a point
  F1 or ?                        The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc closes them)
  Enter or Space, after a game   Play again
  M, or Esc after a game         Back to the menu
  Q                              Quit

When a piece is picked up, the points it can go to are lit, and the geese a
jump would kill turn red under the cursor.

Our choice: where several series of jumps end on the same point, the program
offers the one that kills the most geese.

fox-and-geese --demo lets the computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a
game exactly.
