What survives
-------------

The game. Mū tōrere, or tōrere, is a game of the Māori of the East Coast of
the North Island. Elsdon Best describes it in Games and Pastimes of the
Maori (1925), as it was explained to him by Tuta Nihoniho of Ngāti Porou: “A
board is marked with charcoal with a design resembling an eight-pointed
star”, its centre “termed the putahi, the radiating arms are termed kawai or
tentacles, the design being compared by the Maori to an octopus”. Each
player has four perepere, “small stones so marked that each player’s men may
be readily distinguished”, and “there is no taking or crowning of men, it is
simply a question of blocking your opponent” (Best 1925, 111). Today the
arms are usually written kewai; this book follows that.

The boards. “The board used was sometimes a piece of hewn plank, sometimes a
piece of the inner bark of the totara tree, the inner side of which was
marked with the design while green”, and kept flat with sticks while it
dried. “In some cases diagrams of a temporary nature were marked on the
earth with a pointed stick” (Best 1925, 112).

How old. Nobody knows. Best was told by Mohi Turei of Ngāti Porou, in 1912,
that mū tōrere was the old name of the game, and old Māori told him that so
far as they knew it was a Māori game. But Tuta Nihoniho said that draughts
reached the district in his grandfather’s time; mū is also the Māori name
for draughts, and may come from the English “move”; and the game was known,
Best found, only on the East Coast. “Some natives maintained that it was a
pre-European usage, others denied this and attributed its introduction to
early whalers.” Best himself was “not inclined to view mu torere as an old
time Maori game” (Best 1925, 110-13). Te Ara, the encyclopedia of New
Zealand, gives both views, and adds that tōrere was a popular game on the
East Coast “from the very early years of European contact” (Derby 2013).

One of Best’s informants said the game was also played with one man a side
on a board of six points (Best 1925, 113). The program does not offer that.

What is not known. Best’s account is the one record of the rules from a
player of the game. It leaves open who begins, and how far one rule reaches:
the rule that keeps a man out of the pūtahi. The program offers both
readings of that rule (see below).

The pūtahi beside an enemy
--------------------------

The rules as most accounts give them today:

1.  Each player has four men, on four neighbouring kewai.
2.  In turn, each moves one of his men to the empty point, if it is joined
    to the man’s point.
3.  A man may move from a kewai into the pūtahi only if at least one of the
    two kewai next to it holds a man of the other side.
4.  A player who cannot move has lost.

Rule 3 is not in Best’s words (see the next rule set). We have not found who
first gave it in this form; Wikipedia gives it without a source (Wikipedia
2026), and Cameron Browne reports that Marcia Ascher, in her analysis of the
game Browne (2021), found that “at least two historians’ transcriptions of
its rules simplified out an apparently trivial starting rule, without which
the game became unplayable” (Browne 2021, 2). Without any such rule the
first player wins on his first move: a man whose neighbours are both his own
steps into the pūtahi, and the other side is blocked.

With this rule the game is a draw if both play well. The program’s tests
work out every position (1,180 can be reached from the start) from the ends
back, and the start is drawn; Wikipedia gives the same count and the same
result (Wikipedia 2026).

Our choices:

- White begins. Best’s sample game is opened by the player on 5 to 8; he
  gives no rule.
- The same position with the same side to move, a third time, is a draw. No
  source says how a game that goes round in circles ends.

Mū tōrere after Best
--------------------

The rules exactly as Best gives them from Tuta Nihoniho (Best 1925, 111-12):

1.  “One player has his four men on points 1, 2, 3 and 4, the other player
    puts his four on points 5, 6, 7 and 8.”
2.  “No jumping over an occupied point is allowed, to move a man from one
    point to another it must be moved to the next point, which must be
    unoccupied.” A man may move “either way”.
3.  “A man can be moved to the putahi if it be unoccupied.”
4.  “B cannot open the game by moving either 6 or 7, as they are tapu
    (prohibited) for the time being. He can move 5 or 8 to the putahi.” The
    reason: if he had, “A would have been blocked at the outset”.
5.  The player who is blocked “finds himself piro, or out”, and “has to
    capitulate”.

Read as it stands, rule 4 only forbids the opening move from the inner men;
after that rule 3 lets any man enter the empty pūtahi. But then the game is
soon over: after B opens with 5 and A answers 4 to 5, B must bring his man
out of the pūtahi to 4, and A wins by moving his man on 2, whose neighbours
are both his own, into the pūtahi. The program’s tests find the side that
opens lost in two moves of its own whatever it does.

Tuta Nihoniho’s sample game does not go that way. Replayed by the program,
with the opening player as Black:

    Tuta Nihoniho's game (Best 1925, p. 111), B opening:
      B: kewai 5 to the pūtahi (beside an enemy)
      A: kewai 4 to kewai 5
      B: the pūtahi to kewai 4
      A: kewai 3 to the pūtahi (beside an enemy)
      B: kewai 4 to kewai 3
      A: the pūtahi to kewai 4
      B: kewai 3 to the pūtahi (beside an enemy)
      A: kewai 2 to kewai 3
      B: the pūtahi to kewai 2
      A: kewai 4 to the pūtahi (not beside an enemy)
    The end:
      B --- W --- B        8 --- 1 --- 2
      | \   |   / |        | \   |   / |
      B --- W --- W        7 --- P --- 3
      | /   |   \ |        | /   |   \ |
      B --- W --- .        6 --- 5 --- 4
    B to move: blocked, A has won.

Every man A and B move into the pūtahi comes from beside an enemy, but for
the last, which wins the game: so the sample fits neither reading exactly.
The program offers Best’s words as they stand, as the second rule set, and
the stricter rule as the first.

Our choices: White begins, and a third repetition is a draw, as above.

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start mu-torere, choose a rule set, whom to play against (the computer,
easy, normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side you
play.

  Key                            
  ------------------------------ -----------------------------------------------
  Arrows                         Move the cursor
  Enter or Space                 Move the man under the cursor
  Mouse                          Click a man to move it
  F1 or ?                        The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc closes them)
  Enter or Space, after a game   Play again
  M, or Esc after a game         Back to the menu
  Q                              Quit

The computer knows every position: hard plays perfectly, normal now and then
plays any move that does not lose at once, easy plays at random but takes a
win and avoids a loss one move away.

mu-torere --demo lets the computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a game
exactly.
