What survives
-------------

The game. The morris games are played on a board of points joined by lines.
Each player enters men of his colour on the points, one at a time, and in
most forms then moves them along the lines. Three men of one colour on one
line make a row, a mill. In the smallest game a mill wins; in the larger
ones it lets its maker take one of the other side’s men. R. C. Bell puts
them first among his “games of position” (Bell 1969, 1:91-95).

The names come from the pieces. Friedrich Berger traces merels, French
marelle and mérelles, to the Latin marellus, a little man or game piece, and
the German Mühle, the mill, to mîle, from the Latin miles, the foot soldier,
used for the game around 1215 (Berger 2004, 16). “Morris” is the English
name, and Joseph Strutt guessed at its reason in 1801: “from the moving
these men backwards or forwards, as though they were dancing a morris”
(Strutt 1845).

Ovid. The first words about any rule are Roman, and they are about the game
of three men a side. Ovid, in his Art of Love, lists the games a young woman
should know:

  Parva tabella capit ternos utrimque lapillos,
  in qua vicisse est continuasse suos.

  A smaller board presents three stones each on either side, where the
  winner will have made his line up together.

Ovid’s lines are from book III, 365-366 (Ovid 2001), here and below in A. S.
Kline’s translation. He repeats them almost word for word in the Tristia,
the poems of his exile: “how a small board’s set with three ‘stones’ a side,
and winning rests in keeping them together” (Ovid 2003, bk. II). Nothing in
either passage is about the larger board.

The oldest boards. Bell gives two very early dates, and both rest on H.
Parker’s Ancient Ceylon of 1909 (Bell 1969, 1:92-93; Parker 1909, 577-80):

- Boards cut into the roofing slabs of the temple at Kurna (Qurna) in Egypt,
  which Bell dates to about 1400 BC, the time the temple was built: a board
  for Nine Men’s Morris and, he says, a board for the game of three. Parker
  found three of the designs partly cut away where the slabs were trimmed,
  so they were cut before the stones were laid, and took the others to be as
  old. Berger reports that Parker lists 34 figures from Kurna in all, many
  of them Coptic crosses, one of them inside a merels board, and that Claude
  Wagneur concluded from this that the figures are Coptic. “Certainly they
  cannot be dated,” Berger writes (Berger 2004, 15).
- Two boards on the great stairway at Mihintale in Sri Lanka, which Bell
  dates to the reign of Mahadathika Maha-Naga, AD 9 to 21. Parker reports
  that date from tradition; his own estimate is that the steps were “already
  built by 245 A.D.” and most probably date to the second century or the
  beginning of the first (Parker 1909, 577-78). Berger notes that no source
  for Bell’s dates is given (Berger 2004, 15).

So neither is a safe date. The oldest dated boards Berger knows are Roman:
one on a tile from the last level of the town of Enserune in southern
France, about 100 BC to AD 40, and one cut into a tile of the Thirtieth
Legion at Xanten in Germany before it was fired, so after AD 196. Many more
are scratched into Roman buildings, but cannot be dated, since they may have
been drawn later (Berger 2004, 15-16).

The Vikings. In the Gokstad ship burial in Norway, opened in 1880, lay part
of an oak gaming board, cut on one side for Nine Men’s Morris and on the
other for a game that may have been hnefatafl (Bell 1969, 1:93-94; Viking
Ship Museum, Roskilde n.d.). The burial dates from about the year 900.

The Middle Ages. By the thirteenth century the game was played from Castile
to Novgorod. The Libro de los juegos that Alfonso X of Castile had made in
1283 gives the first written rules of morris anywhere, for nine men with
dice and without, and for three (see below) (Golladay 2007, 623-27). Bell
writes that the game “reached its zenith in the fourteenth century”, that
the codices of the North Italian academies collect morris problems as they
collect chess problems, and that boards for it were cut into the cloister
seats of Norwich, Canterbury, Gloucester and Salisbury cathedrals and
Westminster Abbey (Bell 1969, 1:92-94). From the fourteenth century on,
boxes were made with chess on one side of the lid, Nine Men’s Morris on the
other and backgammon inside (Bell 1969, 1:95).

In England “nine men’s morris” was also cut into the turf. When Titania
describes the ruined summer in A Midsummer Night’s Dream, “The nine men’s
morris is filled up with mud” (Shakespeare, n.d.). Strutt, in 1801, still
saw shepherds play it that way, the lines cut in the ground and a hole for
every point (Strutt 1845).

The board with diagonals. The board of Nine Men’s Morris with its corners
joined is the board of Twelve Men’s Morris. Berger, after H. J. R. Murray,
finds it first in Arabic sources: Murray cites the Kitab al-Aghani, compiled
between 918 and 967, for a house in Mecca kept with sets of chess, nard and
qirq, the Arabic name for the game (Berger 2004, 16, citing Murray). In
southern Africa the game on this board, Morabaraba, is played to this day.

What is not known. Nothing written before Alfonso X gives the rules of any
morris game, and nothing in Ovid goes beyond three in a row. The rules below
come from three kinds of source: Bell’s short accounts (Bell 1969, 1:91-94),
the rules clubs and federations play by now (Welt-Mühlespiel-Dachverband
2008; Mind Sports South Africa 2007), and Alfonso’s book for the game of
three (Golladay 2007). Where they differ, the program offers each.

The board of Nine Men’s Morris: three squares, one inside another, joined at
the middles of their sides. Twenty-four points, sixteen lines of three.

This book names the points as players of Nine Men’s Morris write them:
columns a to g from left to right, rows 1 to 7 from bottom to top, so that
a1 is the bottom left corner and g7 the top right. The smaller boards are
named the same way. The program shows no names but uses them in its
messages.

A mill is three men of one colour on one line of three points. On the board
of nine there are sixteen: four along the sides of each square and four
across. A man moves along a line to the next point.

Nine Men’s Morris, today’s rules
--------------------------------

The game as clubs and tournaments play it. The rules here are those of the
World Mill Association’s leaflet of 2008 (Welt-Mühlespiel-Dachverband 2008,
6) and of Ralph Gasser’s paper of 1996, which solved the game: with perfect
play from both sides, it is a draw (Gasser 1996, 101-3).

1.  Each player has nine men. The board is empty at the start. White begins
    (Gasser).
2.  In turn, each enters one man on any empty point, until all are in.
3.  Then each in turn moves one of his men along a line to the next point,
    if it is empty.
4.  A player who makes a mill, by entering or by moving, takes one of his
    opponent’s men off the board. “Any piece may be taken, except those
    being in a closed Mühle” (Welt-Mühlespiel-Dachverband 2008, 6). A mill
    may be opened by moving a man out of it and closed again by moving it
    back; each time it is closed it takes a man.
5.  “A player who has only 3 pieces left, may leap, in other words he may
    place his pieces on any desired point” (Welt-Mühlespiel-Dachverband
    2008, 6).
6.  “If a player has only 2 pieces left, he has lost the game.” So has a
    player who cannot move (Welt-Mühlespiel-Dachverband 2008, 6).

Two points, Gasser writes, “are subject to debate among Nine Men’s Morris
enthusiasts” (Gasser 1996, 103): whether two mills made at once take one man
or two, and whether a mill may take anything when all the other side’s men
stand in mills. The World Mill Association counts both as regional
deviations it does not accept, without saying what its own rule is
(Welt-Mühlespiel-Dachverband, n.d.). Gasser’s program let two mills take one
man, and let a mill take any man when all were in mills; it seemed unlikely
to him that either choice changes the value of the game. Rainer
Rosenberger’s handbook says the association has since dropped its ban on
taking from a mill (Rosenberger 2016, 32).

Our choices:

- Two mills made by one move take one man, as Gasser.
- When every man of the other side stands in a mill, a mill may take any of
  them, as Gasser.
- The same position with the same side to move, a third time, is a draw.
  Gasser counts a game drawn as soon as a position comes back; the leaflet
  does not say.

Nine Men’s Morris after Bell
----------------------------

Bell’s account (Bell 1969, 1:93):

1.  Each player has nine men, and they “enter them alternately one at a
    time, on any vacant point”.
2.  “Each time a player forms a row or mill of three pieces along a line he
    removes one of his opponent’s pieces from the board, but not one which
    is in a mill.”
3.  When all are entered, each in turn moves a man “to an adjacent vacant
    point along a line”.
4.  “A player blocking all his opponent’s men so that they cannot move, or
    reducing him to two pieces, wins the game.”
5.  “A mill can be made and broken any number of times; an enemy piece being
    captured each time the mill is made. If a player only has three pieces
    in a mill left on the board and it is his turn to move, he must do so
    even if this means losing a piece and the game at his opponent’s next
    move.”

The last rule makes plain that Bell’s game has no flying: a player with
three men moves them along the lines like any other. Strutt’s account of
1801 has none either, and ends the game only when one side “takes off all
his antagonist’s pieces” (Strutt 1845); the program does not offer that
ending.

Our choices:

- Who begins: White, as today. Bell does not say.
- When every man of the other side stands in a mill, a mill takes nothing.
  Bell’s words, “not one which is in a mill”, allow no exception.
- Two mills made by one move take one man. Bell does not say.
- A third repetition is a draw, as above.

The board of twelve: the board of nine with its corners joined along the
diagonals. Twenty lines of three.

With twelve men each, Mind Sports South Africa’s Generally Accepted Rules
(Mind Sports South Africa 2007):

1.  “Each player starts with 12 tokens called ‘cows’.” They are placed in
    turn on empty points; mills count “vertically, diagonally, or
    horizontally”.
2.  A mill shoots one of the opponent’s cows. “A player can not shoot an
    opponent’s ‘three-in-a-row’ cows if there are other cows on the board
    left to shoot.” “Only one of the opponent’s cows may be shot at a time,
    even if two (or more) lines of three-in-a-row are achieved with one
    move.”
3.  When all are placed, a cow moves to an empty point “immediately linked
    and adjacent”. Mills may be broken and made again, and each time a mill
    is made again a cow is shot. But “if a player breaks a line to
    immediately create a new line of three cows, such player may not move
    such cow back to the original junction in his/her next move, if it
    creates a new line.”
4.  “When a player has lost all but three cows”, his cows fly to any empty
    point.
5.  The game is won when the opponent cannot move, or has lost all but two
    cows. “When a player is down to three cows, and neither player is able
    to shoot an opponent’s cow within ten moves, the game is a draw.”

Twenty-four cows fill the twenty-four points. If none is shot while placing,
nobody can move. The rules above do not say what happens then. Rosenberger
gives the game as a draw (Rosenberger 2016, 43); Gábor Gévay and Gábor
Danner, who solved the game on this board with twelve men each under
Gasser’s rules (with perfect play White wins), note that there is no
consensus on such a rule, and that without it the full board is a loss for
White, the side to move (Gévay and Danner 2016).

Our choices:

- A board filled while placing is a draw, as Rosenberger.
- The ten moves are ten of each side, twenty in all, counted from the moment
  a side is down to three cows and started again at every shot.
- Who begins: White.
- A third repetition is a draw, as above.

Berger reports, after Murray, an older rule for this board: men move along
the diagonals but make no mill there (Berger 2004, 15, citing Murray). The
program does not offer it.

The board of Six Men’s Morris: two squares, joined at the middles of their
sides. Sixteen points, eight lines of three.

“Six Men’s Morris was popular in Italy, France, and England during the
Middle Ages but was obsolete by 1600” (Bell 1969, 1:92):

1.  “Each player had six pieces and they were entered alternately, one at a
    time; each player trying to form a row along one of the sides of either
    square.”
2.  “If a player succeeded in this he removed any one of his opponent’s
    pieces.”
3.  “When all the pieces had been entered the game continued by alternate
    moves of a piece along a line to an adjacent empty point.”
4.  “When a player was reduced to two men the game was over.”

Our choices:

- “Any one” of the opponent’s men: a mill takes a man in a mill as readily
  as any other.
- A player who cannot move loses, as in Bell’s Nine Men’s Morris. Bell does
  not say.
- White begins; two mills at once take one man; a third repetition is a
  draw.

Three Men’s Morris after Bell
-----------------------------

      3  +---+---+
         | \ | / |
      2  +---+---+
         | / | \ |
      1  +---+---+
         a   b   c

    + a point. Neighbours on a line are joined.

The board of Three Men’s Morris, with its diagonals, as Bell draws the board
he connects with Kurna (his fig. 78). Eight lines of three.

Bell’s second form of the game (Bell 1969, 1:92):

1.  “Each player had three men of his own colour and entered them in turn on
    any vacant point on the board,”
2.  “and then the game continued with alternate moves until one player
    succeeded in placing his three men in a straight line.”
3.  “As the first player could always force a win by correct play, dice may
    have been used to decide the advantage of first entry.”

Alfonso X’s alquerque de tres, of 1283, is played on the same board with
three men each, six in all: “he who plays first wins if he should know how
to play it well. And the play of it is this: he who should more quickly
place his pieces in a row wins” (Golladay 2007, 626). The first player, the
book goes on, should enter his first man in the middle, and his second so
that the other must block it; then he must block in turn, “and if in this
way he should have placed them so that wherever the other player puts his
remaining piece he loses” (Golladay 2007, 626-27).

Alfonso does not say whether the men move once they are in. Sonja Musser
Golladay, his translator, reads the game as placing only, like noughts and
crosses (Golladay 2007, 607-8, 626). But a game of placing only, three men
each, is a draw with correct play: the program’s tests work out every
position of it, and of Bell’s game, from the end back. Alfonso’s first
player who “wins if he should know how to play it well” fits Bell’s game, in
which the men move: there the first player does win, if he plays well. The
program therefore offers Bell’s game, and takes it to be Alfonso’s too.

Our choices:

- A man moves one step along a line, diagonals included. Bell says only
  “alternate moves”.
- White begins, and may enter his first man anywhere, the middle included;
  none of our sources forbids it.
- A third repetition is a draw.

The board of Bell’s first form: a square of three by three without diagonals
(his fig. 77). Six lines of three.

Bell’s first form (Bell 1969, 1:91-92): “The board contains nine points and
each player has four counters of his own colour. The players place one
counter alternately on a point, and if one player can place three in a
straight line he wins the game.” His figure has no diagonals. It is noughts
and crosses with counters, and as in noughts and crosses correct play from
both sides draws.

Our choices: Bell says nothing of moving the counters, so they are only
placed, and when all eight are down without a row the game is drawn. White
begins.

Other variants
--------------

None of these is in the program.

Nine Men’s Morris with dice. Alfonso X’s book also has a game of nine men
with three dice for the placing. As Golladay translates it: “the first to
play rolls the dice first, if the pips of them should say 6-5-4, 3-3-6,
5-2-2, or 1-1-4, for whichever of these rolls that he may throw, he will
place three pieces in a row and he will remove each time one of the pieces
of the other player. And if he rolls so that he makes two mills he will
remove two pieces” (Golladay 2007, 625-26). Bell describes the same throws
but a different rule: they let the thrower take a man out of an enemy mill
as well as enter one, any other throw only enters a man, and the dice are
put away once all are entered (Bell 1969, 1:94). Golladay calls Bell’s
account incorrect (Golladay 2007, 606). What exactly the lucky throws do is
not clear enough from the text to play.

Alquerque de doze. The game of twelve in Alfonso’s book is not Twelve Men’s
Morris but the game of alquerque: twelve men each on a board of twenty-five
points, capturing by jumping, as in draughts (Golladay 2007, 623-24).

Playing it in the terminal
--------------------------

Start morris, choose a rule set, whom to play against (the computer, easy,
normal or hard, or a friend at the same keyboard) and which side you play.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Key                                   
  ------------------------------------- -------------------------------------
  Arrows                                Move the cursor

  Enter or Space                        Enter a man; pick up a man, then put
                                        it down on a lit point; choose the
                                        man a mill takes

  Esc                                   Let go of the man, or take back a
                                        move whose mill has not yet taken

  Mouse                                 Click a point

  F1 or ?                               The rules in short (F1, ? or Esc
                                        closes them)

  Enter or Space, after a game          Play again

  M, or Esc after a game                Back to the menu

  Q                                     Quit
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The side panel shows how many men each side has on the board and still to
enter. When a man is picked up, the points it can go to are lit; after a
mill, the men it may take turn red.

The computer plays at three strengths. Easy searches two moves ahead and now
and then plays any move at all; normal searches four moves deep; hard
searches as deep as it can in a second and a half. All three keep away from
positions the game has already seen. Three Men’s Morris is small enough to
solve when the game starts, a few thousand positions, and hard plays both
its forms perfectly: as the first player in the moving game it always wins.

morris --demo lets the computer play both sides; --seed N repeats a game
exactly.
